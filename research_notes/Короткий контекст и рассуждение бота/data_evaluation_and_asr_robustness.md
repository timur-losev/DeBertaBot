# Training data, evaluation method and recognition-error robustness for context-dependent spoken commands (state as of October 2026)

Conventions used in these notes: every item under "Cited Findings" carries its source link and the year of the work. Items under "Inferences" are my own reasoning or estimates and are marked as such; they are not published results. "No figure published" means I looked and found none. Numbers were read from the linked pages on 2026-10-10/11; where a page was only seen through a search summary and not opened, that is stated.

## 1. Building multi-turn command data and keeping evaluation independent of the generator

### Takeaway
The published recipe that works with the least label noise is "structure first, wording second": sample the state and the dialogue-act sequence with a deterministic simulator or templates, then let an LLM (or people) rewrite one utterance at a time; whole-dialogue LLM generation is reported to hallucinate slots and needs a verification pass that throws away a large share of the output. Synthetic dialogues recover roughly 95 to 98 % of the benefit of human data in the published DST studies, but text-to-speech test audio is explicitly reported as not a reliable stand-in for human speech, and counterfactual or contrast test sets expose drops (up to 30.8 points of joint goal accuracy) that ordinary held-out sets hide.

### Cited Findings

#### Methods for producing dialogues with state
- Agenda-based user simulation (Schatzmann, Thomson, Weilhammer, Ye, Young; NAACL 2007 short paper) is the classical scripted user simulator used to bootstrap a dialogue system without real users — [ACL Anthology N07-2038](https://aclanthology.org/N07-2038/)
- M2M, "Machines Talking To Machines" (Shah et al., 2018): a simulated user bot and a domain-agnostic system bot self-play to produce dialogue outlines (template utterances paired with their semantic parses); crowd workers then rewrite the wording while keeping the meaning. It needs only a task schema and an API client. Released corpus: 3,000 dialogues in 2 domains — [arXiv 1801.04871](https://arxiv.org/abs/1801.04871)
- Schema-Guided Dialogue dataset (Rastogi et al., 2019/AAAI 2020): over 16k multi-domain conversations in 16 domains (the abstract page does not describe the collection method) — [arXiv 1909.05855](https://arxiv.org/abs/1909.05855)
- MultiWOZ (2018) is the Wizard-of-Oz reference point: about 10k human-human written dialogues collected entirely by crowdsourcing, without professional annotators; no cost figure published on the abstract page — [arXiv 1810.00278](https://arxiv.org/abs/1810.00278)
- SynthDST (Apple, EACL 2024) needs only the schema and a few hand-written templates: an abstract dialogue model defines valid system-user intent transitions, templates cover 22 dialogue acts (11 system, 11 user) with 2 to 4 templates per act, and GPT-3.5 paraphrases at the utterance level. The authors report that dialogue-level and multi-step prompting "caused slot hallucination and merged utterances", which is why they paraphrase one utterance at a time — [arXiv 2402.02285](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.02285v1)
- LUAS (Niu et al., ACL 2024): GPT-4 plays a user simulator and an agent simulator, each steered by intention-specific prompts, with control identifiers for intent and end of conversation; a GPT-4 slot extractor checks that the conversation matches the slot-filling result and inconsistent conversations are regenerated. No human quality review is described — [ar5iv 2405.13037](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2405.13037), [ACL Anthology](https://aclanthology.org/2024.acl-long.473)
- DiaSynth (2024, NAACL Findings 2025) generates dialogues from personas, subtopics and chain-of-thought prompts; quality was judged with automatic metrics only (FED, GPTScore, G-Eval), and the search found no human evaluation in it — [arXiv 2409.19020](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.19020v3)
- "From Schema to State" (Xu et al., EMNLP 2025) generates synthetic dialogues from scenarios, logic flows and utterances via "dynamic complexity prompting" instead of hand-written templates and distils an LLM into a smaller model in two stages; its exact joint goal accuracy was not retrieved (seen through a search summary only) — [conference record](https://staffprofiles.bournemouth.ac.uk/display/conference/518134)

#### Measured quality, biases and data volumes of LLM-generated dialogue data
- SynthDST, MultiWOZ 2.1 / 2.4 joint goal accuracy: zero-shot 39.9 / 45.6; few-shot with synthetic data sized as 1 % of the training set 45.8 / 51.0; few-shot with human-annotated 1 % 45.0 / 52.1; human 100 % 46.0 / 55.2. The paper summarises this as +4 to 5 points over zero-shot and recovery of about 98 % (2.1) and 95 % (2.4) of human-annotated few-shot performance — [arXiv 2402.02285](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.02285v1)
- SynthDST volumes and cost: the 1 %, 5 % and 10 % synthetic sets are about 549, 2,748 and 5,495 dialogues and cost USD 0.38, 1.88 and 3.78 in API calls (four calls per sample); a MultiWOZ-sized set (about 55k dialogues) is stated to cost under USD 40. More synthetic data did not help: 45.8 (1 %) vs 44.9 (5 %) vs 45.4 (10 %) on MultiWOZ 2.1 — [arXiv 2402.02285](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.02285v1)
- SynthDST, value of the LLM paraphrase step: template-only data gave 43.8 joint goal accuracy against 45.8 with LLM rewriting (1 % setting, MultiWOZ 2.1). Human rating of 200 dialogues by four evaluators: mean and median coherence and naturalness above 4 on a 5-point scale; annotation accuracy median 3 on a 3-point scale. Stated weaknesses: inter-slot dependencies, no hallucination safeguard, no human correction step — [arXiv 2402.02285](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.02285v1)
- LUAS volume and effect: 7,556 generated dialogues (102,602 turns) next to 8,438 real MultiWOZ 2.2 dialogues; joint goal accuracy real-only 65.42 vs real plus generated 66.25 on MultiWOZ 2.2, and 77.20 vs 78.20 on MultiWOZ 2.4. Replacing one domain's real data by generated data lowered accuracy in every domain (−0.20 to −1.19, average −0.75 points); slot recall fell 0.32 points on average. The authors name a "distribution discrepancy" between GPT-4 and real dialogues as the reason they use two-stage fine-tuning (generated first, real second) instead of mixing. A generated-only result is not reported and cost is not stated — [ar5iv 2405.13037](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2405.13037)
- DiaSynth: models fine-tuned on its synthetic data beat their base models by 16.47 % on average and capture 90.48 % of in-domain performance on a summarisation downstream task; data quality rose as the generating LLM grew from 3B to 8B parameters — [arXiv 2409.19020](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.19020v3)
- LLM-generated hard negatives need heavy filtering: of 11,080 ChatGPT-generated "hard-negative out-of-scope" utterances, 8,172 passed the first verification step and 3,779 (34 %) the second; 47 were found to be mislabelled in-scope and removed — [arXiv 2403.05640](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.05640v1)

#### Keeping evaluation independent and making it discriminating
- DSTC11 speech-aware track (2022/2023): training audio was TTS only, dev and test had TTS and human speech. Baseline joint goal accuracy: TTS-Verbatim 38.4, Human-Verbatim 31.8, Human-Paraphrased 30.9. The organisers conclude that TTS is not a reliable surrogate for human speech, because the drop from written input is larger for human speech than for TTS — [ar5iv 2212.08704](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2212.08704)
- CoCo (Salesforce, ICLR 2021) builds counterfactual test dialogues by changing the turn-level goal (dropping or adding slots, swapping values) and generating a matching user turn. State-of-the-art trackers lost up to 30.8 points of joint goal accuracy (49.4 % to 18.6 %), whereas paraphrasing alone moved accuracy by at most 2 %; human evaluation found that more than 95 % of generated turns reflect the intended goal; using CoCo data for augmentation improved the best tracker by 5.5 % on the original test set — [arXiv 2010.12850](https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.12850v3), [Salesforce write-up](https://www.salesforce.com/blog/?p=97764)
- Contrast sets (Gardner et al., 2020): the dataset authors themselves manually make small, meaning-changing perturbations of test instances; across 10 datasets model performance was lower on contrast sets by up to 25 % — [arXiv 2004.02709](https://arxiv.org/abs/2004.02709)
- CheckList (Ribeiro et al., ACL 2020): behavioural test matrix of capabilities by test types with templated test generation; in the user study practitioners with CheckList created twice as many tests and found almost three times as many bugs; a team owning an already heavily tested commercial sentiment model still found new actionable bugs — [ACL Anthology](https://aclanthology.org/2020.acl-main.442/)
- CoDA21 (Senel, Schick, Schütze; ACL 2022) is a context-definition alignment benchmark: the model must match a definition to the context in which the defined (hidden) word occurs. It is a test of understanding from context, not a perturbation method for dialogue data — [ACL Anthology](https://aclanthology.org/2022.acl-short.92)
- Simulated recognition noise for training can come from a different recogniser than the one tested: text-level and TTS-ASR augmentations built with a Kaldi system still helped when the test audio was transcribed by Google's recogniser (STS-B, high noise: 82.20 to 85.95) — [Feng et al., Interspeech 2022](https://www.isca-archive.org/interspeech_2022/feng22c_interspeech.pdf)

### Inferences
- (Own reasoning) For a 41-intent command bot the SynthDST layout maps directly: sample a game state, a previous order, the bot's last line and a relation label (new, repeat, correct, refine, confirm, cancel); the deterministic planner gives the gold order; only then is the player's line written, one line at a time, by blind authors. This keeps labels exact, which is the property the published pipelines pay for with verification passes.
- (Own reasoning) The published volumes are small: 549 to 5,495 synthetic dialogues were enough to close most of the gap for in-context DST, and 7.5k generated dialogues added about one point on top of 8.4k real ones. For short two-to-three-turn command exchanges a first set of a few thousand context/utterance pairs is in the range the literature uses; the SynthDST result that 5 % and 10 % were no better than 1 % suggests diversity matters more than count.
- (Own reasoning) Independence rules that follow from the above: (a) test dialogues are written by other authors or other model families than training dialogues, and are human-written or at least human-verified, since none of the LLM pipelines above had a human correction step; (b) the test set contains minimal pairs in both directions: the same utterance under two contexts with different gold orders, and two utterances under the same context; (c) a CoCo-style counterfactual slice in which only the sampled state is changed; (d) an "utterance-only" baseline is reported on the context test, so that a set whose answers are guessable without context is detected; (e) the single-turn criterion keeps being reported unchanged, so that context handling cannot silently cost single-turn accuracy; (f) at least one slice of the voice test is human speech, because DSTC11 shows TTS audio flatters the system.
- (Own reasoning) The 34 % survival rate of LLM-generated hard negatives and the mislabelled-in-scope cases mean generated negatives must be checked against the specification before they enter either training or test.

### Gaps
- No study was found that measures LLM-generated data for short spoken command dialogues (two or three turns with corrections) specifically; all figures above are from MultiWOZ-style booking dialogues.
- No published figure for how many context-dependent examples a fine-tuned encoder classifier needs to learn a "relation to previous turn" label.
- Exact results of "From Schema to State" (EMNLP 2025) were not retrieved.
- I found no 2025 to 2026 study that quantifies lexical or structural bias of LLM-generated dialogues against human ones beyond the LUAS "distribution discrepancy" remark and the SynthDST human ratings.

## 2. Annotation scheme for context and reported inter-annotator agreement

### Takeaway
Reduced dialogue-act tag sets reach kappa of about 0.8 (SWBD-DAMSL 42 tags: 0.80; reduced ISO 24617-2 subsets: 0.81 to 0.82; a Vietnamese ISO corpus: 0.76 Fleiss), while full multidimensional schemes range from 0.55 to 1.00 per dimension. Corrections are a known special case: they are misrecognised more often than other turns and can be detected automatically with error around 16 %; I found no public command corpus with a published agreement figure for a new/repeat/correct/refine/confirm/cancel label.

### Cited Findings
- SWBD-DAMSL: 84 % inter-labeller agreement on the 42-label set, kappa 0.80 (Stolcke et al., 2000) — [arXiv cs/0006023](https://arxiv.org/pdf/cs/0006023)
- The full SWBD-DAMSL scheme has over 200 unique labels; most studies use a reduced set of 42 to 44 labels to get higher agreement and more examples per class — [arXiv 1506.00839](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1506.00839)
- ISO 24617-2 (standard established in 2012) annotates, per functional segment, the communicative function, the dimension, qualifiers, dependence relations and rhetorical relations — [Bunt, Petukhova, Traum, Alexandersson chapter](https://dialogbank.lsv.uni-saarland.de/wp-content/uploads/2015/12/MMI-standards-bookchapter_v06.pdf)
- Reduced ISO 24617-2 subset for conversational agents (Mezza et al., COLING 2018): three of the standard's dimensions (general/task with directives, commissives and information-transfer functions; social obligation management; auto- and allo-feedback). Agreement kappa 0.82 with 3 annotators on 458 Alexa Prize user turns and 0.81 with 2 annotators on 13 socialbot conversations — [ar5iv 1806.04327](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1806.04327)
- A Vietnamese ISO 24617-2 dialogue-act corpus reports Fleiss kappa 0.76 (seen through a search summary) — [ResearchGate record](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/322594152_A_Vietnamese_Dialog_Act_Corpus_Base_on_ISO_24617-2_Standard)
- For the layered DIT++ scheme (ancestor of the ISO standard), dimension-level kappa between 0.55 and 1.00 is attributed to Geertzen et al. (2008), and qualifier assignment above 0.8 for conditionality; both figures were seen only in a search summary of the Bunt et al. chapter, not in the opened text — [Bunt et al. chapter](https://dialogbank.lsv.uni-saarland.de/wp-content/uploads/2015/12/MMI-standards-bookchapter_v06.pdf)
- Geertzen and Bunt (2006) argue that plain kappa is not adequate for hierarchical tag sets, because related tags (for example Answer and Confirm) count as total disagreement, and propose a weighted statistic that uses the tag hierarchy — [ACL Anthology W06-1318](https://preview.aclanthology.org/fix_video/W06-1318.pdf)
- DialogBank, the gold-standard ISO 24617-2 resource, is small: 15 English dialogues (MapTask, TRAINS, DBOX, Switchboard) with 2,360 annotated segments (search summary) — [DialogBank](https://dialogbank.lsv.uni-saarland.de/?p=268)
- Corrections in spoken dialogue (Swerts, Litman, Hirschberg, ICSLP 2000, TOOT system): corrections are misrecognised more frequently than non-corrections but are no more likely to be rejected; they differ prosodically in ways consistent with hyperarticulation, and corrections further from the error are recognised more poorly — [ISCA Archive](https://doi.org/10.21437/ICSLP.2000-344)
- Automatic correction detection (Litman, Hirschberg, Swerts, Computational Linguistics 2006): best feature set lowers classification error from baselines of 25.70 to 28.99 % down to 15.72 % — [ACL Anthology J06-3004](https://preview.aclanthology.org/setup/J06-3004)
- Rephrase detection in a production assistant (Amazon, EMNLP 2021): modelling rephrase detection with dialogue history and implicit signals such as the time gap between turns outperforms pairwise utterance comparison "significantly"; no precision figure was retrieved — [ACL Anthology](https://aclanthology.org/2021.emnlp-main.143)

### Inferences
- (Own reasoning) A per-turn record that the literature supports for a command bot: intent (the existing 41), slots/place reference, grounded referent (which pin, which object), a relation-to-previous label with six values (new, repeat, correct, refine, confirm, cancel), and the antecedent turn it depends on (the ISO "dependence relation" idea). The dialogue act proper can stay coarse (directive, answer/confirm, feedback), in line with the reduced subsets that reach kappa of about 0.8.
- (Own reasoning) With a deterministic simulator the relation label is assigned by construction, so agreement only has to be measured on the human-written test set. A double-annotated sample of 200 to 500 turns is the size at which the published kappa figures above were obtained (458 turns, 3 annotators).
- (Own reasoning) The Swerts/Litman finding predicts the project's own observation: follow-up corrections ("No, here, on my pin") are exactly the turns where the recogniser is worst, so the correction slice should be measured from audio, not from typed text.

### Gaps
- Inter-annotator agreement for repair (reparandum/repair) annotation in Switchboard was not retrieved.
- No public command or robot-instruction corpus with a published agreement figure for a correction/refinement relation label was found.
- The exact per-dimension kappa table for ISO 24617-2 was not opened; the 0.55 to 1.00 range is second-hand.

## 3. Metrics that show whether "understanding what the speaker means" improved

### Takeaway
Joint goal accuracy is criticised because the state is cumulative: one early error makes every later turn wrong, so it under-reports; the proposed replacements all add turn-local credit. For a thresholded command classifier the two errors that matter are the wrong action (misunderstanding) and the unnecessary "say again" (false rejection); the classical result is that their relative cost is data-dependent and differs per dialogue state, and the one controlled human study found that explicitly signalling non-understanding lowered users' sense of task success while a task-related question did not.

### Cited Findings
- Criticism of joint goal accuracy: because the belief state is cumulative, a correct prediction is hard to obtain once one misprediction has occurred, and the metric tends to underestimate by giving no partial credit — [Flexible Goal Accuracy paper, 2022](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2204.03375), [Kim et al., ACL 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.03123v2)
- Plain slot accuracy errs the other way (it is inflated by the many unmentioned slots); relative slot accuracy ignores unmentioned slots and does not depend on the number of predefined slots — [Kim et al., ACL 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.03123v2), [DST survey 2022](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2207.14627)
- Flexible goal accuracy gives partial credit to mispredictions that are locally correct (errors inherited from an earlier turn); average goal accuracy scores only turns with active slots; granular change accuracy (2024) is a later alternative focused on state changes — [arXiv 2204.03375](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2204.03375), [arXiv 2403.11123](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2403.11123)
- The DSTC11 spoken track reports joint goal accuracy as primary and slot error rate as secondary metric — [ar5iv 2212.08704](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2212.08704)
- Rejection threshold as a trade-off (Bohus and Rudnicky, Interspeech 2005): rejecting inputs below a fixed confidence trades misunderstandings against false rejections in a possibly non-optimal way; they estimate the relative costs of the two errors from data and optimise a separate rejection threshold per dialogue state; the estimated costs matched designers' intuitions (conference-room reservation system). No numeric cost ratio was retrieved — [ISCA Archive](https://www.isca-archive.org/interspeech_2005/bohus05_interspeech.html)
- Human error handling (Skantze, 2003, modified Wizard-of-Oz with a real recogniser in the loop): human operators rarely signalled non-understanding; they asked task-related questions that confirmed their hypothesis. That strategy led to better understanding of subsequent utterances, whereas signalling non-understanding led to decreased user experience of task success — [paper PDF](https://www.cs.columbia.edu/~julia/papers/skantz03.pdf), [thesis page](https://www.speech.kth.se/~gabriel/thesis.html)
- Targeted clarification questions generated by rule reached subjective scores comparable to human-generated questions, against generic "please repeat" prompts (Stoyanchev, Liu, Hirschberg, 2014) — [paper PDF](https://www.cs.columbia.edu/speech/PaperFiles/2014/AISB2014_StoyanchevLiuHirschberg_final.pdf)
- Clarification has a cost: in a within-subjects Wizard-of-Oz study with 25 participants the overall search experience degraded with system clarifications (Z = −2.066, p = 0.04) (Ghosh and Shah, 2025) — [JASIST record](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/jinfst/v76y2025i5p819-839.html)
- Robots: participants' trust increased significantly when a robot acknowledged its errors or limitations (survey-based study, 2024) — [arXiv 2403.14293](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2403.14293); a 252-participant trust study supports that prompt correction of a robot's wrong action restores trust (Luo et al., 2021) — [CPS-VO record](https://archive.cps-vo.org/node/85525)
- Embodied instruction following with questions (DialFRED, 2022): binary task success averaged over tasks, a path-weighted success that penalises extra steps, and the number of questions asked are reported together to show the trade-off; 53K human-annotated task-relevant questions and answers — [arXiv 2202.13330](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2202.13330)
- Calibration: modern neural networks are poorly calibrated; expected calibration error measures the gap between confidence and accuracy; temperature scaling (one parameter fitted on held-out data) is often the most effective post-hoc fix (Guo et al., 2017) — [arXiv 1706.04599](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1706.04599); later work warns that expected calibration error estimates can be strongly biased — [arXiv 2106.07998](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2106.07998)
- Rejection quality is reported with threshold-free measures in the out-of-scope literature: area under the ROC curve, false positive rate at 95 % true positive rate, and area under precision-recall. On CLINC-150 a BERT classifier's false positive rate at 95 % goes from 0.121 on general out-of-scope inputs to 0.326 on hard negatives; on Banking77 from 0.205 to 0.639 — [arXiv 2403.05640](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.05640v1)
- CLINC150 (150 intents, 10 domains): classifiers do well on in-scope intents but "struggle to identify out-of-scope queries" (Larson et al., EMNLP 2019) — [ACL Anthology D19-1131](https://aclanthology.org/D19-1131/)
- Recognition-side biasing is reported with precision, recall and F-score on the key phrases next to overall word error rate and speed, which makes over-biasing visible as a precision drop — [TurboBias, 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.07014v2)
- Splitting results by whether the recogniser's best hypothesis matches the transcript is used to show where understanding gains come from (Amazon, 2020: about 19 % of test utterances had a wrong best hypothesis and carried most of the gain) — [ar5iv 2001.05284](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2001.05284)

### Inferences
- (Own proposal) A metric set that maps onto the published ones: (1) turn-level order accuracy given context, reported separately for context-dependent turns and context-free turns; (2) episode success (every turn of a short exchange correct) as the harsh joint-goal analogue, never alone; (3) minimal-pair consistency (both members of a pair correct); (4) false action rate and false rejection rate at the shipped threshold, plus a risk-coverage curve and false positive rate at 95 % acceptance on near-miss chatter; (5) unnecessary-question rate (the bot said "say again" although the typed version of the same line is accepted); (6) time to correct: number of player turns from a wrong action or rejection to the intended order being executed; (7) expected calibration error on recogniser output, not only on typed text; (8) every metric computed three ways: typed, TTS voice, human voice.
- (Own reasoning) Skantze's result argues for replacing the generic "say again" by a task-related question or a hypothesis read-back ("Shoot at your pin?") where one candidate dominates, and for measuring the two kinds of bot reply separately.
- (Own reasoning) Bohus and Rudnicky's per-state thresholds are directly applicable: after the bot has asked a question, or right after an order that has natural follow-ups ("again", "no, here"), a lower acceptance threshold for the expected intents is justified, and the threshold should be tuned from logged outcomes rather than fixed globally.

### Gaps
- No user study was found that directly compares, under matched conditions, a wrong executed action with a clarification question for a voice-commanded game companion or robot and gives a cost ratio.
- No published metric definition named "time to correct" was found; it is my own proposal built on the correction literature.
- I found no public report of the metric set used by in-car voice assistants (no figure published in the sources reached).
- Risk-coverage curves (selective classification) and the calibration benefit of ensembles were not verified against a primary source in this pass.

## 4. Making the classifier robust to recognition errors

### Takeaway
Training on simulated recognition errors is the cheapest measure with consistent published gains, and the gain is largest where data or model capacity is small (up to +21 points with 1 % of the data, about +0.3 points for a full BERT model with full data). TTS-then-recogniser augmentation gives the best accuracy on noisy input but can cost accuracy on clean text, while text-level confusion simulation is nearly as good and does not; a separate transcript-correction model was the least effective of the three in the one head-to-head study. Reading the top five hypotheses instead of one cut classification errors by 12 to 26 % relative.

### Cited Findings
- Head-to-head comparison on ASR-GLUE (Feng et al., Interspeech 2022; BERT-base; test audio from six native speakers, 32k utterances, 91.4 h, three noise levels): TTS-then-recogniser augmentation "always gains the highest accuracy on the data with ASR errors" but lowered clean-text accuracy on some tasks (SciTail 94.74 to 90.79, QNLI 90.73 to 87.42, RTE 68.93 to 63.76). Examples under high noise: SciTail 62.39 to 78.62, QQP 67.77 to 76.18, QNLI 77.15 to 82.12, SST-2 80.84 to 81.92 — [paper PDF](https://www.isca-archive.org/interspeech_2022/feng22c_interspeech.pdf)
- Same study, text-level augmentation (confusion-matrix substitution, or GPT-2/BART trained to produce recogniser-like errors): "more promising" overall, works in most situations without degrading clean-text accuracy in general; none of the three text-level variants dominates. Augmented and original data were mixed 1:1, and the paper states the original clean data cannot be dropped — [paper PDF](https://www.isca-archive.org/interspeech_2022/feng22c_interspeech.pdf)
- Same study, transcript correction before the classifier (GECToR or BART trained to fix transcripts): on half of the tasks the corrected pipeline was similar to or worse than no correction (for example SST-2 high noise 80.84 baseline vs 78.69 with BART correction) — [paper PDF](https://www.isca-archive.org/interspeech_2022/feng22c_interspeech.pdf)
- Humans lose little to the same errors: human accuracy under high noise stayed at 90.13 on SciTail where the model fell to 62.39 — [paper PDF](https://www.isca-archive.org/interspeech_2022/feng22c_interspeech.pdf)
- Confusion-matrix error simulation for dialogue-act classification (Amazon Alexa, 2020; DSTC2 with 27.89 % word error rate): the simulator aligns reference and hypothesis at word level by Levenshtein distance and counts n-gram confusions; it was built from 10,000 transcribed utterances of other domains. Accuracy on recogniser output: BERT-based model 80.76 to 81.05; GloVe model 80.34 to 81.19; small LSTM 76.92 to 80.81. With 1 % of the training data 43.67 to 64.94, with 5 % 71.79 to 77.65, with 75 % 81.05 to 81.27. Confusion-matrix simulation beat uniform random errors (80.98 vs 80.80), and mixing error rates (27.9 %, 20 %, 15 %) with three simulated copies per reference was best. No change to the recogniser and no added latency — [ar5iv 2006.05635](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2006.05635)
- Using the recogniser's five best hypotheses (Amazon Alexa, 2020): relative error reduction of 14.29 % for domain classification (average pooling of per-hypothesis encodings) and 25.55 %, 25.00 % and 11.92 % for intent classification in three domains; on the 19 % of utterances where the best hypothesis was wrong the reduction was 24.67 to 26.23 %; on the other 81 % it was between −0.38 and +4.50 %. Accuracy grows with the number of hypotheses and levels off after four. A classifier fed the true transcript bounds the gain at 27.04 % (domain) and 32.89 to 47.63 % (intent). Naive use (majority vote, sort by score) gave under 2 % — [ar5iv 2001.05284](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2001.05284)
- A fusion network over the hypothesis list gave 21.71 % error reduction for domain classification against a baseline trained on transcriptions (Amazon, 2021) — [Amazon Science](https://www.amazon.science/publications/asr-n-best-fusion-nets)
- Phoneme-level input representations make classifiers more robust to "quasi-oronyms" (sound-alike phrases with different meaning) and "significantly outperform" word-level baselines on noisy transcripts of four datasets and on real Alexa utterances (Fang et al., SIGIR 2020); the abstract page gives no numbers — [Amazon Science](https://www.amazon.science/publications/using-phoneme-representations-to-build-predictive-models-robust-to-asr-errors)
- Size of the problem in benchmarks: on SLURP the best recogniser pipeline was only about 5 % below the gold-transcript result despite 16 % word error rate (2020) — [arXiv 2011.13205](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2011.13205); on Speech-MASSIVE a Whisper-large-v3 cascade loses under two points of intent accuracy for German, Russian and Spanish and more for languages with higher word error rate (2024) — [arXiv 2408.03900](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2408.03900); in spoken MultiWOZ (DSTC11) a tracker fell from 53.8 % joint goal accuracy on written input to 26.1 % on spoken input and recovered only to 30.9 % after retraining on recogniser hypotheses with augmentation (figures from the paper's conclusion; its Table 9 lists different written-input values, 43.1 and 52.8, and the text elsewhere gives 33.5 % for the best baseline) — [ar5iv 2212.08704](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2212.08704)
- DSTC11 participants all used a cascade (recogniser, then tracker); many added an explicit transcript-correction model and TTS-based augmentation; three used Whisper — [ar5iv 2212.08704](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2212.08704)
- Limits of synthetic voices as training speakers (Lugosch et al., 2020, end-to-end speech-to-intent): on Fluent Speech Commands, training on 22 synthetic voices alone reached 94.9 % against 99.1 % with real speech; accuracy rose sharply up to about 15 synthetic voices and then flattened; three real speakers alone already beat the 22 synthetic voices; on Snips, adding synthetic speech raised accuracy from 65.5 % to 71.4 % — [ar5iv 1910.09463](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1910.09463)
- Risk of accepting nonsense, and the fix: classifiers are measurably worse at rejecting hard negatives that share vocabulary with in-scope data (BERT area under ROC 0.968 general vs 0.914 hard on CLINC-150; 0.964 vs 0.810 on Banking77); training with hard negatives restores it (CLINC-150 hard-negative area under ROC 0.914 to 0.988; Banking77 0.810 to 0.996), and training with general out-of-scope data alone does not (0.916 and 0.874) — [arXiv 2403.05640](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.05640v1)

### Inferences
- (Own reasoning) The project already does the audio-level variant (TTS voices through the recogniser). The published evidence suggests adding the cheap text-level variant: build a confusion table from the reference/hypothesis pairs the TTS pipeline already produces plus every real confusion the owner logs ("shoot/should", "pin/opinion", "fire/five", "angle/tango"), and sample substitutions into clean training lines at more than one error rate. Expected size of the gain, by analogy with the Alexa study: small on the full-data ensemble (tenths of a point overall), larger on the rare intents and on exactly the confusable words.
- (Own reasoning) Confusion augmentation teaches the classifier that "Should there." means SHOOT. The paired hard negatives must therefore be lines that contain the confused form in its ordinary meaning ("Should I go there?", "In my opinion we wait", "Give me five") labelled as non-command or as their true intent, generated by a different author than the test negatives and verified by hand, since two thirds of LLM-generated hard negatives failed verification in the published pipeline. The false positive rate at 95 % acceptance on such negatives is the number to watch before and after.
- (Own reasoning) Hypothesis-list input is attractive on paper (12 to 26 % fewer errors, concentrated on misrecognised utterances) but depends on the recogniser exposing several hypotheses; see section 5 for the current state of beam search for this model in sherpa-onnx.
- (Own estimate) Feeding five hypotheses through three DeBERTa models separately would multiply classifier time about five-fold (40 to 70 ms becoming 200 to 350 ms); concatenating them into one input avoids that at the cost of retraining on concatenated inputs. Neither figure is published.

### Gaps
- No published figure for TTS-then-recogniser augmentation on a small-vocabulary command classifier with a modern recogniser (Parakeet or Whisper class); the ASR-GLUE study used a Kaldi system with far higher error rates.
- The numeric results of the phoneme-representation paper (SIGIR 2020) were not retrieved.
- No study was found that measures false accepts introduced by confusion augmentation itself; the hard-negative evidence is from typed out-of-scope data, not from recogniser confusions.
- NoiseQA and STOP were not examined in this pass.

## 5. Fixing errors on the recogniser side for a small vocabulary

### Takeaway
Phrase boosting for the exact model family exists in two places: NVIDIA NeMo's GPU-PB (documented for TDT, measured at +28 to +39 points of key-phrase F-score for a transducer on a 200-phrase list with 2 to 5 % speed cost, on GPU) and sherpa-onnx hotwords for NeMo transducers, which were merged in February 2026 but as of the pages read in October 2026 have an open bug report of "Yeah." or empty output in 20 to 33 % of requests under the required beam-search decoding, with the proposed fix not merged. Over-biasing is measurable: key-phrase precision drops (97 to 90 in one published row) and with very large lists overall word error rate gets worse. Generative correction of transcripts with large language models roughly halves word error rate on in-domain sets but needs models of 0.75B to 13B parameters and fine-tuning, and made LibriSpeech test-other worse.

### Cited Findings

#### sherpa-onnx (the project's runtime)
- The official hotwords page states that only transducer models support hotwords, that the default greedy search does not, and that `modified_beam_search` is required. Parameters: global `hotwords_score` (default 1.5 in the config dump, 2.0 in examples), per-phrase score by appending ` :3.5`, `modeling-unit` (`bpe` for English) and a `bpe.vocab` file. The page reports no word error rate; single-run real-time factors with and without hotwords are close and inconsistent in sign (0.076 vs 0.065, and 0.109 vs 0.106). The page does not name NeMo or TDT models — [sherpa-onnx hotwords docs](https://k2-fsa.github.io/sherpa/onnx/hotwords/index.html)
- Hotwords for NeMo transducers, including TDT models such as Parakeet: pull request #3077, merged 5 February 2026, adds an offline modified-beam-search decoder for NeMo transducers with hotword biasing through the existing context graph, plus a script that generates `bpe.vocab`. The changelog lists it under version 1.12.24. Evidence in the pull request: two single-file runs on Windows with 4 threads, Parakeet TDT 0.6B v2 int8, 7.4 s clip: 0.304 s without and 0.278 s with hotwords; one biasing example ("Phebe" became "Phoebe"); no word error rate — [PR #3077](https://github.com/k2-fsa/sherpa-onnx/pull/3077), [CHANGELOG](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/k2-fsa/sherpa-onnx/master/CHANGELOG.md)
- Open defect: issue #3267 (sherpa-onnx v1.12.25, CPU, Windows, parakeet-tdt-0.6b-v3 int8) reports that `modified_beam_search` returns the hallucinated text "Yeah." or empty text intermittently: about 6 "Yeah." and 4 empty out of 30 requests, while greedy search gave 30 of 30 correct; an empty hotwords file still fails, so the decoding method is the trigger. The issue was still open when read — [issue #3267](https://github.com/k2-fsa/sherpa-onnx/issues/3267)
- Proposed fix, not merged when read: pull request #3657 names three bugs in the NeMo beam-search decoder (no forced frame advance when blank is predicted with duration 0, duration score added to blank candidates, per-frame symbol cap of 10 instead of the greedy decoder's 5); no measurements, and the maintainer asked for a reproducing audio file — [PR #3657](https://github.com/k2-fsa/sherpa-onnx/pull/3657)
- Related changelog entries: "Fix bugs in NeMo transducer modified beam search (#3589)" in 1.13.1 and "Fix non_streaming_server.py crash when using modified_beam_search (#3819)" in 1.13.5; latest version listed is 1.13.8. Whether #3589 resolves issue #3267 is not stated — [CHANGELOG](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/k2-fsa/sherpa-onnx/master/CHANGELOG.md)
- Hotword lists can be set per recognition stream ("OfflineRecognizer supports create stream with hotwords", 1.10.44), so the list can differ per utterance — [CHANGELOG](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/k2-fsa/sherpa-onnx/master/CHANGELOG.md)
- Streaming NeMo transducers still rejected `modified_beam_search` ("Unsupported decoding method", issue #3572); pull request #3895 to add it was open (search summary, not opened) — [issue #3572](https://github.com/k2-fsa/sherpa-onnx/issues/3572), [PR #3895](https://github.com/k2-fsa/sherpa-onnx/pull/3895)
- sherpa-onnx's "homophone replacer" is not a general phonetic post-corrector: it replaces Chinese characters by pinyin rules compiled into an FST and leaves English text unchanged — [homophone replacer docs](https://k2-fsa.github.io/sherpa/onnx/homophone-replacer/index.html)
- The underlying mechanism in k2/icefall and sherpa-onnx is an Aho-Corasick automaton over the tokenised hotwords, applied as shallow fusion inside beam search; a 2025 comparison describes the icefall implementation as CPU-only — [sherpa hotwords docs source](https://github.com/k2-fsa/sherpa/blob/master/docs/source/onnx/hotwords/index.rst), [TurboBias](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.07014v2)

#### NVIDIA NeMo (training-side tooling; GPU)
- NeMo documents three boosting methods: GPU-PB (CTC, RNN-T/TDT and Canary; greedy and beam search; shallow fusion at decoding time; no retraining), Flashlight (CTC only, lexicon mode) and CTC-WS word spotting (CTC and hybrid transducer-CTC). Recommended GPU-PB settings: `context_score=1.0`, `depth_scaling=2.0` for CTC/RNN-T/TDT, `boosting_tree_alpha` tuned on own data. For models that output capitalisation, such as parakeet-tdt-0.6b-v2, phrases previously had to be capitalised in advance; a case-insensitive mode removes that. The page gives no accuracy or latency numbers and does not say whether CPU is supported — [NeMo word boosting docs](https://docs.nvidia.com/nemo/speech/nightly/asr/asr_customization/word_boosting.html)
- An earlier Hugging Face discussion on parakeet-tdt-0.6b-v2 records that the Flashlight decoder does not support TDT and that NVIDIA was building shallow-fusion phrase boosting for RNN-T/TDT — [HF discussion #34](https://huggingface.co/nvidia/parakeet-tdt-0.6b-v2/discussions/34)
- Measured effect of GPU-PB (TurboBias, 2025; key-phrase lists of 200, 893 and 991 phrases; transducer is a hybrid transducer-CTC model, not Parakeet TDT): key-phrase F-score and word error rate on the 200-phrase set, RNN-T greedy 42.5 to 70.4 and 12.8 to 10.7; RNN-T beam 44.2 to 82.9 and 12.8 to 9.6. On the other two sets F-score rose by 6 to 18 points and word error rate moved by 0.1 to 0.7 points. Average slowdown 2 to 5 % in real-time-factor terms — [arXiv 2508.07014](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.07014v2)

#### Measured downsides of biasing
- Precision falls when boosting is applied (example: CTC beam on the 200-phrase set, precision 97 to 90). With the list grown to 20K phrases, F-score fell from 81.1 to 78.4 and word error rate rose from 9.86 % to 11.42 % (still better than the unboosted 12.83 %); the authors lowered the boost weight from 1 to 0.7 to limit false accepts. Attention-decoder models can hallucinate at sentence end under boosting — [arXiv 2508.07014](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.07014v2)
- NeMo's word-spotting variant uses a CTC alignment weight specifically "to prevent false acceptances of boosted words" — [NeMo word boosting docs](https://docs.nvidia.com/nemo/speech/nightly/asr/asr_customization/word_boosting.html)

#### Other recogniser-side options with published numbers
- Aho-Corasick keyword biasing combined with a word-level n-gram language model for a transformer-transducer (Idiap, 2024 to 2025): up to 21.6 % relative improvement in general word error rate with no practical difference in inverse real-time factor; a later version reports entity recognition up to 32 % relative better and overall word error rate up to 12 % relative lower across three datasets (search summary) — [arXiv 2409.13514](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2409.13514)
- Generative error correction from the five best hypotheses (HyPoradise, NeurIPS 2023; 316,881 training and 17,383 test hypothesis/transcript pairs; code and data stated as MIT-licensed): fine-tuned T5-large (0.75B) or LoRA-tuned LLaMA-13B. Best corrected word error rate per set: ATIS 8.3 to 1.7, WSJ 4.5 to 2.2, CHiME-4 11.1 to 6.6, TED-LIUM 3 8.5 to 4.6, Common Voice accent 14.8 to 11.0, Switchboard 15.7 to 14.1. Conventional language-model re-ranking improved most of the same sets by only 0.1 to 1.4 points (for example ATIS 8.3 to 6.9, CHiME-4 11.1 to 11.0) and made Common Voice accent worse (14.8 to 16.0). On LibriSpeech test-other correction made things slightly worse (3.7 to 3.8 or 3.9). Zero-shot use failed for the smaller models (T5 at 0.75B "failed to perform zero-shot error correction"; zero-shot LLaMA-13B gave no improvement on CHiME-4 or Common Voice); few-shot GPT-3.5 gave 15 to 21 % relative. The extracted table's assignment of columns to T5 versus LLaMA was garbled, and the paper's text and table disagree slightly on WSJ and ATIS percentages — [arXiv 2309.15701](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.15701v1)
- Transcript correction with small tagging or sequence-to-sequence models was the weakest of three approaches for downstream understanding in the ASR-GLUE study (section 4) — [Feng et al., 2022](https://www.isca-archive.org/interspeech_2022/feng22c_interspeech.pdf)
- A prompt-biasing approach reports 76.8 % relative reduction in entity error rate with an exact biasing list (2025; search summary) — [arXiv 2506.06252](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2506.06252)

### Inferences
- (Own reasoning) As of the pages read, turning on hotwords for Parakeet TDT in sherpa-onnx means switching the decoder from greedy to a beam-search path that has an open report of losing 20 to 33 % of utterances. The team must reproduce on its own build and audio before relying on it: run the existing voice test set through greedy and through `modified_beam_search` with an empty hotwords file, and compare; only if they match is biasing worth tuning.
- (Own reasoning) The project's observed confusions are single short common words ("shoot/should", "fire/five", "pin/opinion"). Boosting such words is the worst case for over-biasing, because the competing word is frequent in ordinary speech; the published precision drop (97 to 90) was measured on rarer key phrases. Boosting whole command phrases ("shoot there", "open fire", "on my pin", "hold the angle") rather than single words, and measuring false accepts on chatter that contains "should", "five" and "opinion", is the safer design.
- (Own reasoning) Because Parakeet TDT v2 emits capitalised, punctuated text and its token vocabulary is cased, a hotword must be tokenised in the form the model would emit ("Shoot there" at utterance start versus "shoot there" mid-sentence); NeMo solved this with a case-insensitive mode, and I found no statement that sherpa-onnx does the same, so both casings may have to be listed.
- (Own estimate) Cost on CPU: the encoder dominates Parakeet's 150 to 250 ms; beam search with four active paths multiplies only decoder and joiner work. The two single runs in the pull request (0.30 s and 0.28 s for a 7.4 s clip) suggest the overhead is small, but they are not a benchmark and no greedy-versus-beam comparison is published.
- (Own reasoning) Generative correction in the HyPoradise form does not fit a 40 to 70 ms CPU budget (smallest working model 0.75B parameters, fine-tuned, fed five hypotheses). A command-specific alternative with the same idea is to let the classifier itself read several hypotheses (section 4).
- (Own reasoning) NeMo's GPU-PB is useful to the project off-line even if the runtime stays sherpa-onnx: it can be used on the training GPU to estimate how much phrase boosting could fix on recorded test audio before investing in the runtime path.

### Gaps
- No published word error rate or command accuracy for hotwords with Parakeet TDT in sherpa-onnx; no published greedy-versus-beam latency for this model on CPU.
- Whether GPU-PB runs on CPU is not stated in the NeMo documentation.
- Whether sherpa-onnx exposes more than the best hypothesis from the offline recogniser (an n-best list) could not be confirmed from documentation; a search found no statement either way.
- No evidence was found of grammar- or FST-constrained decoding, or of rescoring with an external language model, being available for NeMo transducer/TDT models in sherpa-onnx; the changelog mentions language-model rescoring only for online (streaming) transducers ("Re-implement LM rescore for online transducer", 1.10.25).
- A later pull request, "Support hotwords given as model tokens (modeling_unit=tokens)" (#3980), was listed in search results; its status and relevance to NeMo models were not checked.
- sherpa-onnx's separate keyword-spotting mode (per-keyword threshold and boost, zipformer transducer models) was seen only in third-party documentation and was not verified for use with Parakeet.

## 6. Fine-tuning the recogniser on in-domain speech; licences of models and TTS voices

### Takeaway
No published figure exists for how much audio adapts a Parakeet-class model to a command vocabulary; the nearest evidence is Whisper adapted with synthetic speech only: 31K to 46K synthetic utterances per domain, a single TTS voice and decoder-only LoRA gave 10 to 17 % relative word error rate reduction with about 1 % regression elsewhere, whereas full fine-tuning on one domain made another domain 28.8 % worse. The base models of interest are commercially usable with attribution (Parakeet TDT 0.6B v2 and v3, Canary 1B v2, Kyutai STT: CC-BY-4.0; Whisper and English Moonshine: MIT); among TTS systems Kokoro (Apache-2.0) and Chatterbox (MIT) are permissive, while XTTS-v2 and the F5-TTS weights are not for commercial use and Piper has moved to GPL-3.0 with per-voice dataset terms.

### Cited Findings

#### How much data, and results from synthetic adaptation
- Whisper-base adapted with synthetic speech only (DAS, 2025): text written by Llama3-70b, spoken by one internal TTS voice with no audio augmentation; 44K (music), 31K (weather) and 46K (sports) samples (hours not given); one LoRA adapter per domain on the decoder (rank 32, about 1.2 % extra parameters), encoder frozen. Word error rate on real test sets: music 27.94 to 24.87 (−11.0 %), weather 14.97 to 12.39 (−17.2 %), sports 15.59 to 13.98 (−10.3 %). Regression on LibriSpeech, Fleurs and VoxPopuli between −0.7 % and +1.9 % relative. Full fine-tuning on music reached 23.20 on music but worsened sports by about 28.8 %. Compute cost not reported — [ar5iv 2501.12501](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2501.12501)
- Decoder-only fine-tuning of Whisper on synthetic speech to teach domain vocabulary gave word error rate reductions of 2 to 30 points in low-resource languages, depending on Whisper version (Vicomtech, 2023) — [publication page](https://vicomtech.org/en/rdi-tangible/publications/publication/when-whisper-meets-tts-domain-adaptation-using-only-synthetic-speech-data)
- Single-speaker synthetic TTS data combined with fine-tuning only the final dense layer gives "reasonable improvements" in word error rate for CTC and attention models (2022) — [arXiv 2206.13240](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2206.13240)
- A 2025 evaluation of TTS and voice-conversion augmentation for recogniser training found that adding phonetic diversity to the synthetic data cut character error rate by 12 % and word error rate by 14 % relative to real-only training (search summary) — [arXiv 2503.08954](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.08954)
- Parakeet TDT 0.6B v2 has been fine-tuned on synthetic data for medical terms (AWS and NVIDIA blog): terms with low recall in earlier evaluation were collected into a lexicon, texts written by an LLM, spoken by a neural TTS with accents, noise-augmented; the encoder was unfrozen; the recommended instance has 8 A100 GPUs. The post gives no data volume, no training time and no before/after word error rate — [AWS blog](https://aws.amazon.com/blogs/machine-learning/fine-tuning-nvidia-nemotron-speech-asr-on-amazon-ec2-for-domain-adaptation)
- The Parakeet model cards state that the checkpoints can be fine-tuned on another dataset through NeMo — [parakeet-tdt-0.6b-v2 card](https://huggingface.co/nvidia/parakeet-tdt-0.6b-v2), [parakeet-tdt-0.6b-v3 card](https://huggingface.co/nvidia/parakeet-tdt-0.6b-v3)
- Caution on synthetic-only data: DSTC11 concludes TTS is not a reliable stand-in for human speech; in speech-to-intent training three real speakers beat 22 synthetic voices — [ar5iv 2212.08704](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2212.08704), [ar5iv 1910.09463](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1910.09463)

#### Licences of recogniser models (as read on the model pages)
- NVIDIA Parakeet TDT 0.6B v2: CC-BY-4.0, card states "ready for commercial/non-commercial use"; 600M parameters; trained on about 120,000 hours of English (about 10,000 human-transcribed, about 110,000 pseudo-labelled); average word error rate 6.05 %, LibriSpeech test-clean 1.69 %, test-other 3.19 %; average rises to 6.95 % at 10 dB, 8.23 % at 5 dB and 11.88 % at 0 dB signal-to-noise ratio — [model card](https://huggingface.co/nvidia/parakeet-tdt-0.6b-v2)
- NVIDIA Parakeet TDT 0.6B v3: CC-BY-4.0, "ready for commercial/non-commercial use"; 25 European languages including Russian and Ukrainian; English average 6.34 %, LibriSpeech test-clean 1.93 %, test-other 3.59 % (slightly worse on English than v2 by the two cards' own numbers) — [model card](https://huggingface.co/nvidia/parakeet-tdt-0.6b-v3)
- NVIDIA Canary: canary-1b-v2 is released under CC BY 4.0 — [model card](https://huggingface.co/nvidia/canary-1b-v2); the original canary-1b is CC-BY-NC-4.0 (non-commercial), described by a converter's card as the only Canary variant under a non-commercial licence — [third-party card](https://huggingface.co/handy-computer/canary-1b-gguf); canary-1b-flash is given as CC-BY-4.0 by third-party conversion cards, not confirmed on NVIDIA's own card — [third-party card](https://huggingface.co/handy-computer/canary-1b-flash-gguf)
- OpenAI Whisper: "Whisper's code and model weights are released under the MIT License" — [GitHub README](https://github.com/openai/whisper)
- Moonshine: code and English-language models MIT; models for other languages under the Moonshine Community License, which is non-commercial — [Moonshine assets page](https://huggingface.co/moonshine-ai/moonshine-voice-assets), [original model card](https://huggingface.co/UsefulSensors/moonshine)
- Kyutai STT: "Model weights are licensed under CC-BY 4.0"; stt-1b-en_fr is English and French, about 1B parameters, streaming with 0.5 s delay; the card's only example runs on CUDA and gives no CPU guidance; stt-2.6b-en is English-only with 2.5 s delay — [stt-1b-en_fr card](https://huggingface.co/kyutai/stt-1b-en_fr), [Kyutai announcement](https://kyutai.org/2025/06/19/stt-open-source.html)

#### Licences of TTS systems and voices used to generate data
- Kokoro-82M: Apache-2.0 weights; 82M parameters; v1.0 has 54 voices in 8 languages; trained "exclusively on permissive/non-copyrighted audio data" (public domain, Apache/MIT-licensed audio, and synthetic audio generated by closed TTS models of large providers), a few hundred hours, plus two small CC BY sources; the card says the model is deployed in commercial APIs and states no restriction beyond Apache-2.0 — [model card](https://huggingface.co/hexgrad/Kokoro-82M)
- Chatterbox (Resemble AI): the model card states the MIT licence — [model card](https://huggingface.co/ResembleAI/chatterbox)
- Piper: the original rhasspy/piper repository (MIT) was archived on 6 October 2025; development moved to OHF-Voice/piper1-gpl under GPL-3.0-or-later (secondary source) — [review article](https://www.promptquorum.com/power-local-llm/piper-tts-review). Each voice has its own MODEL_CARD with licensing information and "some voices may have restrictive licenses"; a maintainer-level reply says the LibriTTS-based voice is CC BY (commercial use allowed with attribution) — [Piper discussion #271](https://github.com/rhasspy/piper/discussions/271)
- XTTS-v2: Coqui Public Model License — [model card](https://huggingface.co/coqui/XTTS-v2); secondary sources describe it as non-commercial and note that Coqui shut down in January 2024, so nobody sells a commercial licence — [guide](https://www.promptquorum.com/power-local-llm/local-tts-voice-cloning-piper-coqui-xtts)
- F5-TTS: code MIT; "the pre-trained models are licensed under the CC-BY-NC license due to the training data Emilia" — [GitHub README](https://github.com/SWivid/F5-TTS)

### Inferences
- (Own reasoning) The Whisper results suggest the adaptation recipe least likely to damage general recognition is parameter-efficient and decoder-side. For a transducer the analogue is to adapt the prediction network and joiner (or adapters) rather than the whole encoder; no published Parakeet result confirms this, so it is a hypothesis to test, with LibriSpeech-style regression checks before and after.
- (Own estimate) A command vocabulary of a few hundred phrases, spoken by 20 to 50 TTS voices with noise and speed variation, yields tens of thousands of utterances, the same order as the 31K to 46K per domain in the Whisper study. Expected gain by analogy: 10 to 17 % relative word error rate on in-domain speech; the gain on the specific confusable words may be larger or smaller and is not predictable from the literature.
- (Own reasoning) Fine-tuning Parakeet also means re-exporting to ONNX and re-quantising for sherpa-onnx and re-running the whole voice criterion; compared with a 10-minute classifier run this is the most expensive option per experiment, which supports the owner's "later" for recogniser retraining.
- (Own reasoning, not legal advice) CC-BY-4.0 on Parakeet permits shipping a fine-tuned derivative commercially with attribution. A TTS model whose weights are non-commercial (XTTS-v2, F5-TTS) should not be used to generate training data for a commercial product; Kokoro and Chatterbox are the clean choices; for Piper the GPL applies to the engine code if it is distributed, while audio generated off-line for training is governed by each voice's dataset terms in its MODEL_CARD.

### Gaps
- No figure published for hours or utterances needed to adapt Parakeet TDT (or any FastConformer transducer) to a command vocabulary, nor for catastrophic forgetting after such adaptation.
- Licence of canary-qwen-2.5b was not found in this pass; canary-1b-flash was confirmed only through third-party cards.
- The text of the Coqui Public Model License and the per-voice Piper MODEL_CARD files (for example VCTK- or LJSpeech-based voices) were not opened.
- Licences of speech datasets usable for accent coverage (Common Voice, VCTK, LibriTTS, Fluent Speech Commands, Google Speech Commands, SLURP) were not verified against primary pages; one benchmark site states Fluent Speech Commands is for academic research only — [third-party listing](https://www.codesota.com/browse/1/few-shot-image-classification/195).

## 7. Using dialogue context inside recognition and understanding

### Takeaway
Context helps at both stages, with modest published gains on the recognition side and large ones on the understanding side: contextual language-model rescoring with system dialogue acts gave 7 % relative word error rate reduction; biasing towards an ontology-derived word list cut errors on those words by about 40 % relative; and adding dialogue history to an intent model raised accuracy from 70.7 % to about 94 % in one end-to-end study. The mechanics needed for per-turn biasing exist in the project's runtime (per-stream hotword lists), subject to the beam-search caveat in section 5.

### Cited Findings
- Contextual language-model rescoring for goal-oriented assistants (Amazon; Shenoy, Bodapati, Kirchhoff, 2021): using system dialogue acts, multi-turn context and the bot's own definition gave 7 % relative word error rate reduction over non-contextual utterance-level neural rescorers. This arXiv version was withdrawn in favour of an extended one (arXiv 2104.11070) — [arXiv 2103.10325](https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.10325)
- Tree-constrained pointer generator biasing with lists taken from the dialogue system's ontology, on LibriSpeech and DSTC data (2022): around 40 % relative reduction in error rate on the biasing words — [arXiv 2205.09058](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.09058v2)
- Google (Aleksic et al., Interspeech 2015) frames the same need: the n-grams a user is likely to say depend on dialogue state and on the options just shown, and are often poorly covered by the base language model; the abstract page gives no figure — [ISCA Archive](https://www.isca-archive.org/interspeech_2015/aleksic15_interspeech.html)
- Setting bias words per dialogue turn, such as yes/no vocabulary after a yes/no question or date expressions when a date is expected, is described in a patent (no measurements) — [USPTO 12682186](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/12682186)
- End-to-end spoken language understanding with dialogue history (2021): encoding the preceding utterances as a BERT-based history vector raised intent accuracy from 70.7 % (current-utterance acoustic features only) to about 94 % (search summary of the paper's Table 4) — [arXiv 2108.08405](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2108.08405)
- Intent classification with a window of prior user and system messages (BERT-CNN, Brazilian Portuguese, 2024) reports substantial improvements over the isolated-utterance baseline; numbers not retrieved — [arXiv 2411.06022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.06022v1)
- Dialogue-act classification for open-domain agents (2020): a short reply such as "Oh, yeah" carries different labels depending on the previous turn, so preceding utterances and system state are added to the model — [arXiv 2005.13804](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2005.13804)
- Dialogue-state-specific rejection thresholds (2005) are a third way of using context, at the decision stage — [ISCA Archive](https://www.isca-archive.org/interspeech_2005/bohus05_interspeech.html)
- Runtime support: sherpa-onnx lets a stream be created with its own hotword list ("OfflineRecognizer supports create stream with hotwords", 1.10.44); NeMo's GPU-PB has a per-stream variant for greedy label-looping RNN-T/TDT decoding — [sherpa-onnx CHANGELOG](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/k2-fsa/sherpa-onnx/master/CHANGELOG.md), [NeMo word boosting docs](https://docs.nvidia.com/nemo/speech/nightly/asr/asr_customization/word_boosting.html)
- The Parakeet TDT 0.6B v2 model card describes audio input only and mentions neither a text-prompt input nor word boosting — [parakeet-tdt-0.6b-v2 card](https://huggingface.co/nvidia/parakeet-tdt-0.6b-v2)

### Inferences
- (Own reasoning) Cheapest first step consistent with the evidence: put the context into the classifier input rather than into the recogniser. Concatenate a short structured prefix (previous accepted order, whether the bot just asked "say again" or a question, whether a pin exists) with the transcript and retrain; a 10-minute run per model makes this an afternoon's experiment, and the published gains for history-aware intent models are far larger than those for context-aware recognition.
- (Own reasoning) Second step: context-dependent acceptance. After a rejected or wrong order the prior for "the next utterance is a correction or repeat of a command" is high, which justifies both a lower threshold for the intents compatible with the last order and a phonetic fallback restricted to that expected set (section 8).
- (Own reasoning) Third step, only after the sherpa-onnx beam-search issue is cleared on the team's build: per-utterance hotword lists derived from game state (place names actually present, "pin" only when a pin exists, the expected answers after a bot question). Limiting the list to what is valid in the current state is itself the protection against over-biasing that large static lists lack.
- (Own reasoning) A context feature can be learned as a shortcut ("after SHOOT always predict SHOOT"); the minimal-pair and utterance-only controls from section 1 are what detect this.

### Gaps
- No published result isolates "bias the recogniser with the expected answer after a system question" as the only variable and reports its effect.
- No published result on context-aware recognition for push-to-talk game commands or on joint recogniser-plus-classifier training with game state.
- The numbers of the extended Amazon paper (arXiv 2104.11070) were not retrieved.
- Text-prompt conditioning of the recogniser with the previous turn (as offered by Whisper-class and Canary-class models) was not verified against primary documentation in this pass; the Whisper README page read here does not describe it.

## 8. Phonetic or fuzzy matching at the classifier input as a safety net

### Takeaway
The building blocks are well described (phoneme conversion of the transcript, edit distance weighted by a phone-confusion matrix, matching against an entity or command list), and the literature agrees on the design points: weight substitutions by acoustic similarity, and sum over alignments rather than taking the single best; but I found no published false-accept measurement for phonetic matching against a small command lexicon, and Double Metaphone itself was designed for name lookup and ranks poorly.

### Cited Findings
- Matching recogniser output to an entity list through phonemes (spelling correction by rewriting non-autoregressive lattices, 2024): wordpieces are converted to phonemes and matched against entities; considering all phonetic alignments leading to a match gives better results than the single lowest-cost alignment — [arXiv 2409.16469](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.16469v1)
- Plain Levenshtein distance gives equal weight to every phone insertion, deletion and substitution, which "reduces the system performance" because acoustically similar and dissimilar phones cost the same; substitution costs taken from a phone confusion matrix fix this (2022) — [arXiv 2206.04305](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2206.04305); the same argument for weighted edit distance in alignment — [arXiv 2305.07389](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2305.07389)
- The false-accept trade-off is stated qualitatively: tuning phonetic entity resolution to tolerate dropped or added phonemes "is a balancing act, since we don't want our system to hallucinate sounds that don't exist in the original utterance" (2019) — [ACL Anthology D19-3011](https://aclanthology.org/D19-3011.pdf)
- Parsing intent at the phoneme level with an error model built from a phonetic confusion matrix, to handle sound-alikes and homonyms, is described in a patent (no measurements) — [USPTO 10216725](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/10216725)
- Double Metaphone: produces a primary and an alternate code per word; systems compare all code pairs and combine by maximum, average or weighted average, with thresholds set empirically (patents) — [USPTO 12045561](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/12045561). Its own documentation says it was designed for, and works best with, lists of proper names rather than generic text, and that it provides minimal ranking ability — [Double Metaphone article](https://www.codeproject.com/KB/string/dmetaphone6.asp)
- Phoneme-level input representations improve downstream classifiers on noisy transcripts (Amazon, SIGIR 2020), which is the learned counterpart of rule-based phonetic matching — [Amazon Science](https://www.amazon.science/publications/using-phoneme-representations-to-build-predictive-models-robust-to-asr-errors)
- sherpa-onnx's built-in post-recognition replacer works on pinyin and Chinese characters only, so nothing ready-made exists in the runtime for English — [homophone replacer docs](https://k2-fsa.github.io/sherpa/onnx/homophone-replacer/index.html)

### Inferences
- (Own reasoning) The owner's four failures are all near in phoneme space: "should" and "shoot" differ in the vowel and the final stop's voicing; "open five here" and "open fire here" differ in one consonant; "my opinion" and "my pin is" share the stressed syllable; "tango" and "the angle" overlap after resegmentation. A grapheme-to-phoneme conversion plus confusion-weighted edit distance against the few hundred canonical command phrases would rank the intended phrase first in each case; Double Metaphone's coarse codes are not needed and its lack of ranking is a drawback.
- (Own reasoning) To keep false accepts down, the matcher should be a fallback, not a pre-filter: invoke it only when the classifier is below threshold, require the whole utterance (not a substring) to be within a normalised distance of a command phrase, restrict candidates to intents plausible in the current state, and route the result through the same "wrong action versus rejection" accounting as the classifier. Its accepted outputs are also free training data for the confusion table in section 4.
- (Own estimate) Cost: a grapheme-to-phoneme lookup for under ten words and a few hundred weighted edit distances is well under a millisecond on a desktop CPU; no latency figure is published for this use.
- (Own reasoning) The evaluation that the literature lacks has to be built locally: a list of near-miss ordinary sentences containing the confusable words, a threshold sweep, and the false accept rate at the chosen operating point reported next to the recovered-command rate.

### Gaps
- No published benefit or false-accept figure for phonetic matching of transcripts against a small command lexicon in a command system (smart home, robot, car or game) was found.
- Phonetic or acoustic-neighbour embeddings (for example arXiv 2412.02164) and "synthetic multi-pronunciation" trie biasing (arXiv 2508.17796) were found by title only and not read; their relevance is unverified.

## 9. Live evaluation with real speakers

### Takeaway
Published spoken test sets used for this kind of question are small in speakers (six speakers for ASR-GLUE; 97 for Fluent Speech Commands), and the statistics literature warns that utterances from one speaker are correlated, so confidence intervals must be computed by resampling speakers, not utterances. Legally, voice may count as biometric data in some jurisdictions and consent must be opt-in and specific; keeping audio on the device and logging only transcripts and outcomes is the low-risk design, but the regulator text itself was not read in this pass.

### Cited Findings
- Statistical testing: the ordinary bootstrap is misleading when utterances are dependent, for example several utterances from the same speaker; the blockwise bootstrap resamples non-overlapping blocks and gives a consistent variance estimate for the word error rate difference between two systems (Meta, Interspeech 2020) — [arXiv 1912.09508](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1912.09508)
- Practice example: 1,000 bootstrap resamples for 95 % confidence intervals per speaker group, and within-speaker differences between two systems (2022) — [arXiv 2211.00089](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2211.00089)
- Sizes of published human-voice test material: ASR-GLUE used six native speakers reading the test data at three noise levels (32k utterances, 91.4 h) — [Feng et al., 2022](https://www.isca-archive.org/interspeech_2022/feng22c_interspeech.pdf); Fluent Speech Commands has 30,043 utterances from 97 speakers with 248 phrasings of 31 intents — [arXiv 1904.03670](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1904.03670); DSTC11 recruited crowd workers through Amazon Mechanical Turk to read and to paraphrase user turns (number of speakers not stated) — [ar5iv 2212.08704](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2212.08704)
- A few real speakers are worth more than many synthetic voices: three real speakers beat 22 synthetic voices as training data for speech-to-intent; beyond 40 real speakers adding synthetic voices gave an insignificant +0.07 % — [ar5iv 1910.09463](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1910.09463)
- The recogniser's own noise sensitivity gives a reference for microphone and background variation: Parakeet TDT 0.6B v2 average word error rate 6.05 % clean, 6.95 % at 10 dB, 8.23 % at 5 dB, 11.88 % at 0 dB signal-to-noise ratio, 6.32 % on 8 kHz telephony-style audio — [model card](https://huggingface.co/nvidia/parakeet-tdt-0.6b-v2)
- Corrections and repeats are the hardest live turns: corrections are misrecognised more often than other turns (2000) — [ISCA Archive](https://doi.org/10.21437/ICSLP.2000-344)
- Learning from live use without annotation: production assistants mine the user's own reformulations (rephrases after a failed turn) as implicit feedback to find defects and train rewriting, with context-aware rephrase detection outperforming pairwise comparison (Amazon, 2020 to 2021) — [arXiv 2010.12251](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2010.12251), [ACL Anthology](https://aclanthology.org/2021.emnlp-main.143)
- Legal framing in games: Unity's voice-moderation product notes that in some jurisdictions voice data may be considered biometric data, describes the game developer as the controller and Unity as processor under GDPR, ties recording to consent, states that declining voice features must not block playing the game, and notes that some regions require consent while others require notice and opt-out — [Unity privacy overview](https://docs.unity.com/ugs/en-us/manual/safe-voice/manual/privacy/privacy-overview), [Unity consent text](https://unity.com/legal/biometric-consent)
- Consent under GDPR must be freely given, specific, informed and unambiguous; silence or pre-ticked boxes do not qualify; metadata attached to recordings (timestamps, device identifiers, location) is protected too (compliance article, not a regulator) — [Way With Words](https://waywithwords.net/blog/how-does-gdpr-apply-to-speech-datasets)
- The regulator-level reference in the EU is EDPB Guidelines 02/2021 on virtual voice assistants, version 2.0 adopted 7 July 2021, which applies both the GDPR and the e-Privacy Directive to voice-command services; its specific recommendations on retention and on-device processing were not opened — [EDPB PDF](https://www.edpb.europa.eu/system/files/2021-07/edpb_guidelines_202102_on_vva_v2.0_adopted_en.pdf)

### Inferences
- (Own estimate, binomial approximation, independent utterances) Precision of an accuracy estimate: at 90 % true accuracy, 200 utterances give about ±4.2 points at 95 % confidence, 500 give ±2.6, 1,000 give ±1.9; at 95 % accuracy, 300 utterances give about ±2.5 points. For rare events the "rule of three" applies: zero wrong actions in 300 utterances bounds the wrong-action rate below about 1 % at 95 % confidence.
- (Own estimate) Because utterances from one speaker are correlated, the effective sample is smaller: with 10 speakers and 100 utterances each and an intra-speaker correlation of 0.1, the design effect is about 11, so 1,000 utterances behave like roughly 90 independent ones. More speakers with fewer utterances each is the better use of a fixed budget: 20 to 30 speakers with 40 to 50 utterances each gives a usable first estimate; comparisons between two bot versions should be paired (same audio through both) and tested per speaker.
- (Own reasoning) Coverage plan in the absence of published guidance: stratify the speaker list by accent group and by microphone class (headset, desktop microphone, laptop array), keep it fixed across bot versions, and report results per stratum as well as pooled; add noise at 10 and 5 dB to the recorded audio to reuse it for robustness checks, in line with the degradation the model card reports.
- (Own reasoning, not legal advice) Lowest-risk logging design: opt-in screen separate from the licence agreement; audio never leaves the machine; the uploaded record contains the transcript, classifier scores, the order executed, the next utterance within a few seconds (to detect corrections) and coarse hardware information, with no account identifier; a visible off switch and deletion request path. Stored audio for a recorded test panel is a different activity and should rest on written consent from each speaker stating use for testing and training.
- (Own reasoning) The implicit-feedback idea transfers directly: an utterance followed within a few seconds by "no", by a repeat, or by a near-identical line is a labelled failure candidate; counting these gives a live "time to correct" without any annotation.

### Gaps
- No published guideline was found for the minimum number of speakers or utterances for a command-understanding test; the sample-size figures above are my own calculations.
- No public account was found of how a small game studio collected voice-command telemetry and under which legal basis.
- The EDPB guidelines' concrete recommendations were not read; national rules outside the EU (for example US state biometric laws) were not researched.
- Licences of public accent-diverse speech corpora were not verified (see section 6 gaps).
