## 1. Multimodal deixis and reference resolution (speech + pointing / gaze / cursor)

### Takeaway
Since 1980 the working recipe is: read the pointer at the moment the deictic word is spoken, and otherwise fall back on a short decision list (deliberate gesture, then the persistently selected object, then the only type-compatible visible object, then an explicit name). In simple command tasks that decision list resolved every reference in the published corpora, and even naive recency did well; theories keyed to the form of the expression (it / this / that) help mostly when there is no pointing signal, and without gesture data they resolve only about half of references.

### Cited Findings
- **Bolt 1980, "Put-That-There" [V].** The pointer is sampled when the deictic word occurs: "Where the feed-back cursor is residing on the screen at the time the spoken 'there' occurs becomes the spot", and the spoken "there" "serves as a 'voice button' for the x,y cursor action of the pointing gesture". "That" is "defined as whatever is pointed out". In "Put that there" each deictic is "functionally a when to read the x,y coordinate of pointing". Pointing hardware was a wrist-mounted magnetic sensor cube (Polhemus ROPAMS); the recogniser took up to five words per utterance. Bolt notes users naturally pause before "there". — [Bolt 1980, MIT](https://www.media.mit.edu/speech/papers/1980/bolt_SIGGRAPH80_put-that-there.pdf)
- **Oviatt, DeAngeli and Kuhn 1997 (CHI), pen + speech on a map, 871 constructions [V].** 19% of constructions were multimodal, 63.5% speech only, 17.5% pen only. Spatial-location commands were the only type more likely to be multimodal than unimodal (51–77% of the time) and made up 86% of multimodal constructions. Only 14% of multimodal constructions were "point and speak". In sequential constructions the pen came first 99% of the time; mean lag from end of pen to speech onset 1.4 s; 70% of lags within 2 s, 88% within 3 s, 100% within 4 s. Only 41% of multimodal constructions contained a spoken deictic (59% did not); 96% of deictics were "here", "there", "this" or "that". For sequential deictic constructions the pen mark preceded the deictic 100% of the time, average lag 1.1 s. — [CHI 97 paper](https://chi1997.acm.org/proceedings/paper/slo.html)
- **Oviatt 1999, "Ten myths" [V-s].** Myth 2 is that speak-and-point is the dominant integration pattern; the article argues that algorithms specialised for deictic-point relations have limited practical use. — [CACM](https://cacm.acm.org/research/ten-myths-of-multimodal-interaction)
- **Kaur et al. 2003 (ICMI), gaze + speech, "Move it there" task [V-s].** The fixation that identifies the object occurs on average 630 ms before speech onset, range 150–1200 ms across subjects; within-subject variance small, between-subject variance large. Taking the fixation nearest the onset of "Move" as the designator gave about 95% accuracy for all subjects. — [NJIT record](https://digitalcommons.njit.edu/fac_pubs/14266)
- **Kehler et al. (SRI, Wizard-of-Oz travel map, pen + speech), decision list [V].** A "simple algorithm can be articulated which handles all of the data without making reference to the type of referential expression used": first narrow by the content of the expression ("the museum" needs a museum), then "if there was a simultaneous gesture to an object, then that object is the referent; otherwise the referent is the currently selected object". Distribution facts from the same corpus: "it" always referred to a selected, immediately preceding antecedent; arguments left unsaid (the bare command "Information") almost always referred to the selected, immediately preceding object when no gesture was used; "here" was only used with a gesture; "there" was used for all types of selected referents; unselected objects were never referred to with a pronoun without a gesture. The authors warn that this is "a product of the simplicity of the tasks". — [Kehler, Martin, Cheyer, Julia, Hobbs and Bear, "On Representing Salience and Reference in Multimodal Human-Computer Interaction" (preliminary study)](https://www.isi.edu/people-hobbs/wp-content/uploads/sites/58/2023/11/multimodal-aaai.pdf); final version, not read in full: [Kehler 2000, AAAI](https://mlanthology.org/aaai/2000/kehler2000aaai-cognitive)
- **Kehler's four rules as quoted verbatim by Williams et al. 2016 [V].** "1) If the object is gestured to, choose that object. 2) Otherwise, if the currently selected object meets all semantic type constraints imposed by the referring expression ... choose that object. 3) Otherwise, if there is a visible object that is semantically compatible, then choose that object (this happened three times; in each case there was only one suitable object). 4) Otherwise, a full NP (such as a proper name) was used that uniquely identified the referent." Williams et al. note rule 1 breaks when the gesture target is ambiguous and rule 3 when the expression is ambiguous. — [Williams et al. 2016, HRI](https://hrilab.tufts.edu/publications/williamsetal16hri.pdf)
- **Huls et al. 1995, as described by Kehler et al. [V].** Salience of an entity = sum of weights of "context factors", each decaying over time; linguistic factors (major constituent, subject, nested, relation) and perceptual factors (visible, selected, indicated by a simultaneous pointing gesture); weights set by trial and error; the most salient entity meeting the type constraints of the command wins, regardless of expression type. The simultaneous-gesture weight is set large enough that nothing can outrank it. All compared methods, including "a rather naive recency-based strategy", did very well, which Kehler et al. read as "a lack of difficulty in the problem". Exact counts were lost in PDF text extraction. — [Kehler et al.](https://www.isi.edu/people-hobbs/wp-content/uploads/sites/58/2023/11/multimodal-aaai.pdf)
- **Chai et al., tiered greedy resolution, as described by Williams et al. [V].** Tiers Gesture, Focus, Visible, Others; score of (expression, entity) = P(entity chosen from its tier) × P(tier given the form of the expression) × binary compatibility (type, properties, name, gestured-at-when-uttered); bind greedily down the tiers; handles several expressions and gestures in one utterance. — [Williams et al. 2016](https://hrilab.tufts.edu/publications/williamsetal16hri.pdf); likely original: [Chai, Prasov, Qu, JAIR](https://jair.org/index.php/jair/article/view/10466)
- **GH-POWER (Williams et al. 2016), Givenness Hierarchy without gesture [V].** Search plans by form: in focus ("it") → FOC; activated ("this", "that", "this N") → ACT then FOC; familiar ("that N") → ACT, FOC, FAM; uniquely identifiable ("the N") → ACT, FOC, long-term memory. Coding guideline: targets of concurrent gestures or sustained gaze are at least "activated"; anything mentioned earlier in the dialogue is at least "familiar". Evaluation on 32 human-robot and 110 human-human task utterances (translated from German, disfluencies removed, 16 objects): 45.92% of parser-found references resolved correctly, 55.96% after discarding parser artefacts. Failure shares: 10.26% needed gesture information the system did not use; 10.60% referred to non-discrete entities such as regions; 4.97% plurals; 4.64% task-start context; 3.31% idioms; 1.32% salience scoring; 8.94% other. — [Williams et al. 2016](https://hrilab.tufts.edu/publications/williamsetal16hri.pdf)
- **GazePointAR (CHI 2024) [V-s].** Wearable AR assistant that replaces the pronoun in a spoken query ("What is this?") with a description of what gaze, a pointing gesture and conversation history single out, then sends the rewritten query to a large language model; 12-participant lab study plus a first-person diary study; participants found pronoun queries natural but sometimes counter-intuitive. No resolution-accuracy figure retrieved. — [arXiv 2404.08213](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.08213)
- **Demonstratives and reachability [V-s].** Coventry et al. 2023 (Nature Human Behaviour; 29 languages, over 1,000 speakers) report the same mapping of demonstratives to reachable versus non-reachable space across all languages; addressee position also matters in some languages. Caldano and Coventry 2019: objects at the same location were called "this" when the pointing hand could reach them. — [Coventry et al. 2023](https://ueaeprints.uea.ac.uk/93546/1/s41562_023_01697_4.pdf); [Caldano and Coventry 2019](https://ueaeprints.uea.ac.uk/id/eprint/71541/)
- **Gestural versus symbolic deixis (Levinson 1983; Fillmore 1997) [V-s, secondary sources].** A deictic is "gestural" when it needs audio-visual information (a point) to be understood, "symbolic" when general knowledge of the speech situation is enough; "here" encodes near the speaker, "there" away from the speaker; motion verbs carry deixis of their own ("come here" is movement toward the speaker). — [Wikipedia: Deixis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deixis); [Marburg handout](https://www.uni-marburg.de/de/fb10/iaa/institut/personen/ruehlemann/deixis.pdf)
- **How hard the learned version is: SIMMC 2.0 multimodal coreference [V-s].** Best DSTC10 system (GRAVL-BERT, graph network + VL-BERT) reports F1 0.783; the second-placed UNITER ensemble 73.3% in the official evaluation; the official baseline was 36.6% on the development set. One DSTC10 paper found text-only coreference models with good object representations can avoid a vision component. — [GRAVL-BERT, COLING 2022](https://aclanthology.org/2022.coling-1.22); [UNITER-based system](https://arxiv.org/abs/2112.03521)

### Inferences
- Mapping to the bot (crosshair = pointer, pin = persistent selection, named markers = visible objects, last place = discourse recency). A decision list in Kehler's order is the natural rule-based design: (1) an explicit closed-vocabulary place wins and its content constrains everything else; (2) a spoken "here / there / this / that" binds to the crosshair sample; (3) with no place word, the standing pin plays the role of Kehler's "currently selected object"; (4) then the last place mentioned, with time decay as in Huls et al.; (5) then a single type-compatible candidate in view; (6) otherwise ask or do less. Cost: a few comparisons per utterance, microseconds.
- Sampling time. Bolt samples at the deictic word; Oviatt shows pointing is finished before the word (lag about 1.1–1.4 s for pen); Kaur shows the relevant fixation leads speech by roughly 0.15–1.2 s. For a held push-to-talk key this argues for taking the crosshair sample at the deictic word's timestamp, or in a short window ending there, and never at key release; with no deictic word, the sample at speech onset is the safer one. This needs word timestamps from the recogniser and a ring buffer of crosshair hits.
- The crosshair is always pointing at something, so it is not automatically a deliberate gesture. Oviatt's 59% of multimodal constructions without any spoken deictic means "no 'here' was said" does not prove "no pointing was meant". Evidence that a crosshair sample is deliberate would have to come from elsewhere: a deictic word, type compatibility (an enemy character under the crosshair for "shoot"), dwell before speech, or a freshly placed pin. Which of "enemy under crosshair" and "standing pin" outranks the other for a bare "shoot" is a design decision the literature does not settle.
- Reading "here" and "there" by verb (inference from the gestural / symbolic split and from Kehler's "here only with gesture, there for any selected referent"): with come / regroup verbs "here" is the speaker's position; with verbs whose target cannot sensibly be the speaker (shoot, look, check, throw) "here" and "there" are the pointed spot; with stay / wait / hold "here" and "there" are most plausibly the addressee's current position; "there" with an unusable crosshair sample falls back to the last place mentioned, then the pin.
- Regions matter: 10.6% of GH-POWER's references were to non-discrete entities. "That hole", "that corner", "over there" name regions, not markers, which is exactly where a closed place vocabulary plus a single hit point runs out.

### Gaps
- No published study was found on the timing between a spoken deictic and mouse aim in a first-person game; pen and gaze figures are the nearest analogues.
- No quantified study was found on how often English speakers mean the pointed spot, their own position or the addressee's position with "here" / "there" in imperatives; only the theoretical distinction and the reachability experiments.
- Oviatt 1999 full text returned HTTP 403; GazePointAR accuracy numbers and the 2024 paper on LLM-assisted speech-plus-pointing selection in VR (arXiv 2410.21091) were not read.
- Exact counts in Kehler et al. and Huls et al. were not recoverable from the PDF.

## 2. Under-specification and defaults; asking versus assuming

### Takeaway
Published systems fill an unsaid argument from a small ordered list of contextual anchors (what is selected, what the speaker looks at, where speaker and agent stand) and treat "ask or assume" as an expected-cost decision with two thresholds, in which the cost of a wrong action sets how sure the agent must be. The evidence that defaults carry real weight is strong (success more than halves without them); the evidence on exactly when to ask comes mostly from large-model systems.

### Cited Findings
- **Unsaid arguments resolve to the selected object [V].** In Kehler et al.'s corpus, phonetically unrealised arguments ("Information") almost always referred to the selected, immediately preceding object when there was no gesture. — [Kehler et al.](https://www.isi.edu/people-hobbs/wp-content/uploads/sites/58/2023/11/multimodal-aaai.pdf)
- **CraftAssist (Minecraft assistant, ACL 2020) pushes defaults into the interpreter [V].** Internal nodes "are not generally required to have children; it is the job of the interpreter to deal with under-specified programs like a BUILD with no arguments". The location type is one of COORDINATES, REFERENCE_OBJECT, AGENT_POS, SPEAKER_POS, SPEAKER_LOOK, and nodes carry a "contains_coreference" flag. — [Srinet et al. 2020](https://aclanthology.org/2020.acl-main.427.pdf)
- **MARCO (MacMahon et al. 2006, route instructions in virtual indoor worlds) [V-s].** With inference of implicit actions the agent reached the destination in 61% of trials (humans 69% on the same 786 instructions); executing only the explicit actions dropped success to 28%. The authors conclude implicit-action inference is essential for poor instructions and matters for many highly rated ones. — [MacMahon et al., AAAI 2006](https://www.cs.utexas.edu/ftp/qsim/papers/MacMahon-aaai-06.pdf)
- **Horvitz 1999, expected-utility thresholds [V].** eu(A|E) = p(G|E)·u(A,G) + [1 − p(G|E)]·u(A,¬G); act when p(G|E) exceeds the threshold p* where action and inaction have equal expected utility. Adding dialogue as an option gives two thresholds: one between doing nothing and asking, one between asking and acting; a higher cost of acting wrongly raises the threshold for action. Related principles: "Employing dialog to resolve key uncertainties ... considering the costs of potentially bothering a user needlessly"; "Minimizing the cost of poor guesses about action and timing ... including appropriate timing out"; "A preference for 'doing less' but doing it correctly under uncertainty". — [Horvitz, CHI 1999](https://erichorvitz.com/chi99horvitz.pdf)
- **Skantze 2007, grounding decisions [V-s].** Four grounding actions by confidence band: accept, display understanding, clarify, reject. Thresholds are usually hand-set by intuition; the paper derives them from cost functions that weigh dialogue efficiency, the consequences of task failure and information gain, estimated per dialogue context. It cites Bohus and Rudnicky (2001): false acceptances cost more than false rejections. — [Skantze, SIGdial 2007](https://aclanthology.org/2007.sigdial-1.35)
- **KnowNo (Ren et al., CoRL 2023), conformal prediction over a large model's options [V].** The model proposes four candidate next steps plus "an option not listed here"; conformal calibration (400 scenarios) yields a prediction set, and the robot asks whenever the set has more than one option. Multi-step tabletop: task success 0.74 with help against 0.38 with no help, at a step-wise help rate of 0.58; mobile manipulation: 0.76 against 0.51, help rate 0.67. Help is 10–24% lower than baselines at equal success. Ambiguity types tested: attribute, numeric, spatial, preferences, Winograd-style reference and unsafe actions. — [arXiv 2307.01928](https://arxiv.org/html/2307.01928v1)
- **DialFRED (Gao et al. 2022) [V-s].** A questioner-performer agent that may ask reached 33.6% success on unseen validation against 18.3% for a passive instruction follower; the dataset has 53K human-annotated task-relevant questions and answers. — [arXiv 2202.13330](https://arxiv.org/abs/2202.13330); [Amazon Science summary](https://amazon.science/blog/teaching-virtual-robots-to-follow-natural-language-instructions)
- **Marge and Rudnicky 2015 [V-s].** When a request is referentially ambiguous people prefer to include specific visual information in the disambiguating reply; when it is impossible to execute they prefer to volunteer an alternative plan. The 2019 follow-up (TeamTalk) selects recovery strategies with a nearest-neighbour learner; user-specific models were most efficient. — [SIGdial 2015](https://aclanthology.org/W15-4604/); [ACM TiiS 2019](https://doi.org/10.1145/3237189)
- **AmbiK (ACL 2025) [V-s].** 1,000 pairs of ambiguous and unambiguous kitchen instructions, sorted into human preferences, common-sense knowledge and safety; built because ambiguity-detection methods had been tested on different datasets. Model results were not retrieved. — [arXiv 2506.04089](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.04089)
- **Repetition means "you did not act" [V].** In Raux and Nakano's corpus 45.6% of omission corrections (the operator failed to react) were exact repetitions of the previous instruction, against 6.4% of non-corrections. — [Raux and Nakano, SIGdial 2010](https://www.cs.utep.edu/nigel/dynamics/raux-sigdial10.pdf)
- **Classical accounts [R].** Centering ranks the entities of the previous utterance and prefers continuing the current centre; Grosz and Sidner keep a stack of focus spaces tied to the task structure; Roberts' "question under discussion" says each utterance is interpreted as addressing the currently open question; the information-state approach implements this as a stack of open issues plus update rules; Clark and Brennan's least collaborative effort says speakers leave out whatever the common ground supplies; Clark, Schreuder and Buttrick found listeners resolve an under-specified demonstrative to the perceptually most salient candidate given mutual knowledge. — [Grosz, Joshi, Weinstein 1995](https://aclanthology.org/J95-2003/); [Grosz and Sidner 1986](https://aclanthology.org/J86-3001/); [Roberts 2012](https://semprag.org/article/view/sp.5.6); [Larsson and Traum 2000](https://doi.org/10.1017/S1351324900002539); [Clark and Brennan 1991](https://doi.org/10.1037/10096-006); [Clark, Schreuder, Buttrick 1983](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0022-5371(83)90189-5)

### Inferences
- "Shoot, shoot, shoot" with a standing pin is two published patterns at once: an unsaid argument that should resolve to the selected object (Kehler), and a repetition that signals the first attempt produced no action (Raux and Nakano). A rule that treats an immediate repetition of an unexecuted order as "apply the default now" is cheap and matches both.
- A per-intent default table is the practical form of "sensible defaults": each intent lists its required slots and an ordered fallback chain (explicit word, deictic + crosshair, pin, last place, bot's current target, speaker position, bot position). Rules only; no run-time cost.
- A workable rule-based version of Horvitz and Skantze with three bands: assume and announce ("Firing on your pin") when the default is fresh and the action cheap to undo; do the smaller action and offer the rest ("Aiming at your pin. Fire?") in the middle band; ask only when the two best readings lead to different outcomes. If the two candidate places are within a few metres of each other, or both imply the same target, a question buys nothing.
- Signs that a default is unsafe (own synthesis): the action is irreversible or spends a scarce resource (grenade, ammunition, a jump down, fire that reveals position); the default referent is stale (old pin, place from several orders ago); a precondition fails for the default (no line of sight, not reachable); or the default would contradict a standing constraint such as "hold fire". False acceptance costs more than false rejection for these, as Bohus and Rudnicky found for dialogue.
- Announcing the assumed referent in the acknowledgement is Skantze's "display understanding": it costs no turn and gives the player a one-word correction path.

### Gaps
- No published rule of the form "ask only if the top two readings differ in outcome" was found; it is derivable from the expected-utility framing but the wording is the researcher's.
- Larsson's thesis on issue-based dialogue management (question accommodation) was not retrieved; the QUD description above rests on Roberts and on Larsson and Traum, both recalled.
- How voice assistants choose "sensible defaults" in production is not documented in sources reached this session.
- AmbiK model results were not retrieved.

## 3. Situated instruction following in research: representations, context, failure types

### Takeaway
Benchmarks map language either to low-level action sequences (ALFRED, TEACh), to an executable logical form with explicit anchors for speaker and agent (CraftAssist), or to formal goals. Their error analyses agree that failures concentrate in finding and grounding the referent and in physical access, with language structure a smaller but real share.

### Cited Findings
- **ALFRED (2019/2020) [V].** 25k natural-language directives with expert demonstrations; maps instructions plus egocentric vision to action sequences for household tasks; the abstract says the baseline "performs poorly". — [arXiv 1912.01734](https://arxiv.org/abs/1912.01734)
- **TEACh (2021) [V].** Over 3,000 human-human interactive dialogues for household tasks in simulation; three benchmarks; evaluates dialogue understanding, grounding and execution. — [arXiv 2110.00534](https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.00534)
- **Minecraft Collaborative Building Task (2019) [V].** Architect instructs Builder through chat; 509 conversations with game logs. — [Narayan-Chen et al., ACL 2019](https://aclanthology.org/P19-1537/)
- **CraftAssist Instruction Parsing (2020) [V].** 6,693 annotated instruction-parse pairs (4,532 from prompts, 2,161 from interactive play). Target: a tree with action types Build, Copy, Dance, Spawn, Resume, Fill, Destroy, Move, Undo, Stop, Dig, FreeBuild, plus Noop and memory read/write; sub-trees for location, reference object, repeat and stop condition. About 14% of utterances fell outside the grammar (OtherAction). Players in interactive play give shorter instructions with simpler execution. — [Srinet et al. 2020](https://aclanthology.org/2020.acl-main.427.pdf)
- **HandMeThat (NeurIPS 2022 Datasets track) [V].** 10,000 episodes; the robot watches a human pursue a goal, then gets an ambiguous instruction naming a sub-goal that must be resolved from physical state and the human's inferred goal; offline and online reinforcement-learning baselines "perform poorly"; no numbers in the abstract. — [arXiv 2310.03779](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.03779)
- **Overcooked with language, Hierarchical Language Agent (AAMAS 2024) [V].** A capable large model ("Slow Mind") for intention reasoning and talk, a lightweight model ("Fast Mind") for macro actions, and a reactive policy ("Executor") for atomic actions, motivated by large-model latency being unfit for real-time play; human studies favour it over slow-only and fast-only baselines; no latency figure in the abstract. — [arXiv 2312.15224](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.15224)
- **PUBG Ally (Krafton with NVIDIA ACE) [V-s].** A voice-commanded teammate running on-device speech recognition, a 2-billion-parameter small language model (Mistral-NeMo-Minitron-2B) and text-to-speech, with a separate gameplay layer for fast reactions; reported to fit GPUs with 8 GB of video memory; no latency figures found; reports say challenges remain before it is a commercial feature. — [NVIDIA developer blog](https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/how-krafton-built-pubg-ally-a-co-playable-character-powered-by-nvidia-ace/)
- **ALFRED failure breakdowns [V-s].** Embodied Concept Learner: grounding or target-not-found 36.4% (seen) and 28.5% (unseen) of failures; blocking or inaccessible object 31.3% and 39.8%. Prompter on unseen validation: language-processing errors 32.89% (FILM 24.54%), goal object not found 28.01% (FILM 26.07%), interaction failures 16.20% (FILM 8.54%). Categories differ between papers. — [ECL, arXiv 2304.03767](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2304.03767); [Prompter, arXiv 2211.03267](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2211.03267)
- **RoboAgent (2026) on EB-ALFRED [V-s].** About half of failures came from the visual grounding stage and about 18% from the instruction parser mishandling complex sentences. — [arXiv 2604.07774](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.07774)
- **TEACh behaviour analysis [V-s].** Behaviour cloning reproduces low-level demonstration errors rather than intent, and no tested approach correctly grounds information-requesting queries. — [arXiv 2210.04443](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2210.04443v2)
- **FILM (ICLR 2022) [V-s].** Modular: a language module turns the instruction into sub-tasks once at the start, then semantic mapping, a search policy and a deterministic policy; 24.46% success, 8.17 points above prior work; a re-implementation describes the language part as a trained component whose output is converted into an action plan by a rule-based component. — [arXiv 2110.07342](https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.07342); [CEUR re-implementation](https://ceur-ws.org/Vol-3596/short13.pdf)
- **SIMMC 2.0 [V-s].** See section 1: coreference F1 0.73–0.78 for the best large multimodal models against a 0.37 baseline. — [GRAVL-BERT](https://aclanthology.org/2022.coling-1.22)

### Inferences
- The bot's order {intent, place, direction} is the same shape as a CraftAssist action node with a location child. CraftAssist's AGENT_POS / SPEAKER_POS / SPEAKER_LOOK are direct counterparts of bot position, player position and crosshair; the pin has no counterpart there and is closest to Kehler's "selected object".
- The 14% out-of-grammar share in CraftAssist is a useful prior: even with a designed grammar, roughly one utterance in seven asks for something the agent cannot represent, so an explicit "cannot do that" path is part of the design, not an edge case.
- Common failure types across these analyses: wrong or missing referent (about 26–36%), cannot reach or interact (16–40%), language structure (18–33%). "Wrong default" is rarely counted separately, because most benchmarks give fully specified instructions.

### Gaps
- Baseline success numbers for ALFRED and TEACh, and anything on CVDN, IGLU, MineDojo / Voyager and Talk the Walk, were not verified this session and are left out. From memory only: CVDN and Talk the Walk are navigation dialogues where localisation from language is the bottleneck; Voyager has a large model write code skills into a library; none is a small-model or rule-based system.
- HandMeThat and Hierarchical Language Agent numbers were not retrieved beyond the abstracts.
- No benchmark was found that measures short spoken commands to a combat teammate with a crosshair and a marker.

## 4. Language to plan, neuro-symbolic: what is light enough

### Takeaway
The dependable pattern is "language to a small formal target, then a deterministic executor": large models are weak planners on their own but good translators, and the translation step has small-model and grammar-based variants that reach roughly 76–98% exact match on short in-domain commands. Accuracy drops sharply out of domain, and the residual errors are mostly wrong arguments, not wrong structure.

### Cited Findings
- **Large models as planners [V-s].** Valmeekam et al. 2023: the best model (GPT-4) averaged about 12% success across planning domains; Blocksworld performance collapses when names are obfuscated. The LLM-Modulo position paper argues such models cannot plan or self-verify and should propose candidates to external verifiers. — [arXiv 2305.15771](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2305.15771v1); [arXiv 2402.01817](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.01817v3)
- **LLM+P (2023) [V-s].** The model translates the problem into PDDL, a classical planner solves it, and the plan is translated back; it gives optimal solutions for most problems where models alone fail to give feasible plans. — [arXiv 2304.11477](https://www.arxiv.org/abs/2304.11477)
- **SayCan (2022) [V].** Skill choice = the model's estimate that a skill helps × a value function's estimate that the skill will succeed from the current state; 84% planning success and 74% execution success on 101 instructions. — [SayCan project page](https://say-can.github.io/)
- **Lang2LTL (CoRL 2023) [V].** Modular pipeline: referring-expression recognition (98.01 ± 2.08%), grounding of those expressions to landmarks (98.20 ± 2.30%), "lifted" translation to a temporal-logic template; full system 81.83% in 21 unseen cities. The lifted dataset has 2,125 unique formulas from only 47 templates, adapted from 15 mission patterns. Most common error of the fine-tuned translators: an unknown template with valid syntax. On 52 robot commands it grounded all 52 (40 satisfiable, 12 unsatisfiable), where Code as Policies gave acceptable executions for 23. Stated limits: no pronoun or coreference handling, cannot tell duplicate landmarks apart. Zero-shot transfer to other domains: 49.40% and 78.28%. — [arXiv 2302.11649](https://arxiv.org/html/2302.11649v2)
- **NL2TL (2023) [V-s].** Fine-tuned T5-base (220M) and T5-large (770M); exact-match accuracy 97.52% on model-assisted test data and 90.12% on manually written instructions. A 2025 follow-up (GraFT) re-measures NL2TL end to end at roughly 44–63% on the CW, GLTL and Navi benchmarks under its mixed-training setups, well below those in-domain figures. — [arXiv 2305.07766](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2305.07766); [GraFT, arXiv 2512.16814](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2512.16814)
- **GraFT (ICML 2025), grammar-forced decoding [V].** Restricts the valid output tokens at each step to those the temporal-logic grammar allows; improves end-to-end accuracy by 5.49% and out-of-domain accuracy by 14.06% on average over prior methods. — [arXiv 2512.16814](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.16814)
- **RoboCup@Home command understanding, neural parser (2019) [V].** Commands from the competition's grammar-based generator are parsed to a logical form with 89% accuracy on unseen commands; anonymising entities makes the target easier to learn and often trivially executable. — [arXiv 1907.01115](https://arxiv.org/abs/1907.01115)
- **RoboCup@Home, rule-based (2012) [V-s].** Schiffer, Hoppe and Lakemeyer: grammar-based parse followed by planning to resolve ambiguities and ask for clarification, aimed at the task with ambiguous or faulty spoken input. — [ICAART 2012](https://www.scitepress.org/Papers/2012/37073)
- **CraftAssist parsers [V].** Exact-match on full trees: GRU model 50.08%; DistilBERT encoder + recursive decoder 59.58%; DistilBERT + seq2seq 60.74% overall, 76.06% on interactive-play instructions and 52.49% on prompted ones. The true parse is in the top 2 beams 67.7% of the time and in the top 15 75.7%. Of 363 errors, 125 are only span mistakes and 199 have the right tree but wrong leaves; next most common are confusing whether a location is the action's target or a reference object, and repeat / stop conditions. — [Srinet et al. 2020](https://aclanthology.org/2020.acl-main.427.pdf)
- **Small on-device function-calling model (FunctionGemma, 270M parameters) [V-s].** Google reports 58% accuracy on its "Mobile Actions" evaluation before fine-tuning and 85% after. No latency figures were found. — [Google blog](https://blog.google/technology/developers/functiongemma/); [fine-tuning guide](https://ai.google.dev/gemma/docs/mobile-actions)
- **Robust rule-based parsing of spoken commands [V-s].** Lison: a parser for situated spoken dialogue that handles ill-formed and misrecognised utterances by selectively relaxing grammar rules, then ranks interpretations with a discriminative model that uses context. — [Lison](https://prints.vicos.si/publications/153/robust-processing-of-situated-spoken-dialogue)
- **Structured English to temporal logic by rules [R].** Kress-Gazit, Fainekos and Pappas map a controlled English grammar to temporal-logic specifications from which a controller is synthesised; no language model involved. — [Kress-Gazit et al. 2008](https://doi.org/10.1163/156855308X344864)

### Inferences
What each route needs, what it costs and what supports it. Run-time figures are the researcher's estimates unless a source is named; none of the cited papers publishes CPU latency.

| Route | Needs | Run-time cost (estimate) | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Decision list for referents and defaults | Rules, a short memory | Under 1 ms | Resolved all references in Kehler's and Huls' simple-task corpora; MARCO 61% vs 28% without implicit actions |

- The structure these systems reveal: recognising and grounding the referring expressions is the easy, near-solved part on a closed vocabulary (98%), choosing the right template is the hard part (81.8% end to end), and the number of templates needed is small (47). For a 41-intent bot with a handful of temporal wrappers, the template set can be enumerated by hand.
- CraftAssist's error profile (most errors are wrong leaves, and the right parse is often second in the beam) supports keeping two or three candidate readings and letting situation checks pick, instead of trusting the top reading.

- Behaviour-tree generation from language was not verified this session. From memory only, the published systems fine-tune language models of several billion parameters to emit a tree description; the researcher knows of no rule-based or sub-100M-parameter published variant.
- HuRIC and the FrameNet-based parsers built on it could not be verified (search budget); from memory, HuRIC annotates a few hundred robot commands with frames and spatial roles and its parser is a chain of small statistical taggers, but no figure is given here.
- CCG and dependency-rule parsers for robot commands (Matuszek, Artzi and Zettlemoyer, Tellex's G3) were not re-checked; a recalled survey pointer: [Tellex et al. 2020, "Robots that use language"](https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-control-101119-071628).
- No published CPU latency for any small parser on short commands was found.

## 5. Compound, conditional and timed commands: the smallest formalism

### Takeaway
Command languages that had to be both spoken by people and executed by machines converge on the same small core: a single task frame with a start condition, an optional end condition and a label, plus a separate list of temporal links between labels. Game and robot grammars add an action sequence, a loop and a stop condition. A few dozen templates cover thousands of distinct temporal commands.

### Cited Findings
- **Battle Management Language grammar (Schade and Hieb 2006) [V].** An order is S → B* C_Sp* C_T*: basic task expressions, spatial coordination, temporal coordination. A basic expression is "Verb Tasker Taskee (Affected|Action) Where Start-When (End-When) Why Label (Mod)*". Start-When is either "start Qualifier Point_in_Time" (absolute, for example "start nlt", not later than) or "start Qualifier Action", where Action is the label of another task, "a relative point in time, e.g. at the start of a particular action (whenever this may occur)". End-When is optional. Temporal coordination is a separate statement, for example "start at-the-end-of label_3_12 label_3_11". Where is "at Location" or a route "from Location to Location via Location". Mod carries manner such as formation or speed. The authors moved to verb frames because a flat who-what-where-when-why list was not an explicit grammar. — [Schade and Hieb, SIW 2006](https://c4i.gmu.edu/pdfs/06S-SIW-068%20Spring%202006%20BML%20Grammar.pdf)
- **CraftAssist logical form [V].** A command is an "action_sequence" list; an action may carry "repeat" (FOR a count, or ALL) and "stop_condition", which "essentially allows the agent to understand 'while' loops (for example: 'dig down until you hit the bedrock' or 'follow me')". Control commands Stop, Resume and Undo are first-class actions. In annotation, composite commands were split by workers into individual commands. Stop conditions are a named weak point of the parsers "due to the wide variety of possible stop condition". — [Srinet et al. 2020](https://aclanthology.org/2020.acl-main.427.pdf)
- **RoboCup@Home command generator [V].** Commands come from context-free grammars with wildcards filled from configuration files; it generates sentences for the general-purpose and enhanced general-purpose service robot tasks. The 2019 parsing paper describes it as a probabilistic grammar in three categories of increasing complexity; the task is meant to include multi-step commands, commands with erroneous information and under-specified commands; a 2023 team paper notes that in practice object information is given beforehand, so the competition rarely requires checking whether a command is complete. — [GPSRCmdGen](https://github.com/kyordhel/GPSRCmdGen); [arXiv 1907.01115](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1907.01115); [Groningen summary](https://www.rug.nl/news/2011/11/20111115-bashickendorf); [arXiv 2309.14425](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2309.14425)
- **Mission specification patterns (Menghi et al. 2019) [V].** A catalogue of 22 patterns for mobile-robot missions, each with a temporal-logic template; derived from 245 mission requirements in the robotics literature and evaluated on 441 real-world requirements and 1,251 specifications. — [arXiv 1901.02077](https://arxiv.org/abs/1901.02077)
- **Few templates cover many commands [V].** Lang2LTL: 47 templates, adapted from 15 of those patterns, generate 2,125 unique formulas. — [arXiv 2302.11649](https://arxiv.org/html/2302.11649v2)
- **Property specification patterns (Dwyer et al. 1999) [R].** 92% of 555 collected specifications matched one of a small set of patterns (absence, existence, universality, precedence, response) with a scope (globally, before, after, between, until). — [Dwyer et al. 1999](https://doi.org/10.1145/302405.302672)
- **Trigger-action programming (Ur et al., CHI 2014) [V-s].** In a 318-person study trigger-action rules could express most behaviours people wanted from a smart home; in a 226-person test inexperienced users quickly learned to write rules with several triggers or actions. — [Ur et al. 2014](https://doi.org/10.1145/2556288.2557420)
- **Corrections arrive around sequences [V-s].** Thierauf et al.: verbal corrections occur before, during and after verbally taught sequences of tasks. — [Thierauf et al., ACM THRI](https://hrilab.tufts.edu/publications/thierauf2023thri)

### Inferences
- The smallest representation that covers the owner's cases is the existing order plus four optional fields and a bounded queue (own synthesis from the two grammars above):
  - `start`: now | on player signal ("on my go") | after N seconds | when event
  - `until`: event | N seconds | next order (default)
  - `manner`: closed set of constraints that hold for the whole order ("quietly", "stay low")
  - `then`: the next order, queue depth two or three
  - plus "cancel", which clears the queue and any pending start condition
- Events come from a closed list the game can evaluate (enemy seen, arrived at place, taking fire, timer, player signal). This is one trigger, one end condition and a sequence: the Start-When / End-When / Label core of the military grammar and the action sequence / stop condition of CraftAssist. In pattern terms it covers sequenced visit, wait, simple reaction and a maintained constraint.
- Parsing can stay rule-based: split the transcript on a closed list of connectives ("then", "and then", "after that", "when / once / as soon as X", "until X", "on my go", "in N seconds"), run the existing intent classifier and place matcher on each clause, attach the wrapper. The bot's existing "one order queued on my go" is already the `start: on signal` case.
- "And" is the risky connective: it can join two orders ("go there and hold") or two arguments ("check the door and the window"). CraftAssist's observation that models confuse a location's role is the same problem. Treating "and" as a sequence only when both sides contain an intent verb is a conservative rule.
- Execution is a small state machine or a behaviour-tree sequence with wait-for-condition and time-limit decorators, which the engine's behaviour trees already provide; run-time cost is negligible.

### Gaps
- The share of mission requirements covered by Menghi's 22 patterns was not retrieved (the PDF host failed); the 92% figure for Dwyer's patterns is recalled, not re-checked.
- Radio brevity codes and fire-command formats (alert, direction, description, range, method, command to fire; "at my command") could not be sourced this session; they are the military counterpart of "on my go" and deserve a check.
- The later C-BML standard and the NATO final report were not read; the grammar above is the 2006 proposal.
- No accuracy figure exists for rule-based clause splitting on spontaneous spoken commands.

## 6. Preconditions and self-knowledge: "cannot comply because", and the bot's own words as context

### Takeaway
The best-documented design is a short ordered list of acceptance checks run before executing, each tied to a specific spoken excuse, with the failed precondition itself reused as the explanation; people prefer an alternative to a bare refusal. The agent's own utterances enter the context the same way the user's do: whatever it just mentioned is available for the next reference.

### Cited Findings
- **Briggs and Scheutz 2015, felicity conditions [V].** Five checks: "Knowledge: Do I know how to do X?"; "Capacity: Am I physically able to do X now?"; "Goal priority and timing: Am I able to do X right now?"; "Social role and obligation"; "Normative permissibility". Demonstration: told to walk forward at a table edge the robot answers "Sorry, I cannot do that as there is no support ahead", then "But, it is unsafe", and complies after "I will catch you". The explanation is the failure reason the goal manager stored (failureReason(..., ahead(noSupport))); with no specific reason the fallback is "Sorry, I cannot do that". No quantitative evaluation in the paper. — [Briggs and Scheutz, AAAI Fall Symposium 2015](https://cdn.aaai.org/ocs/11709/11709-51307-1-PB.pdf)
- **Affordance scoring [V].** SayCan multiplies language relevance by a value function's estimate that a skill can succeed from the current state. — [SayCan](https://say-can.github.io/)
- **Unsatisfiable commands detected formally [V].** Lang2LTL's robot demonstration included 12 unsatisfiable commands among 52 and grounded all correctly, which lets the planner report infeasibility. — [arXiv 2302.11649](https://arxiv.org/html/2302.11649v2)
- **People volunteer alternatives [V-s].** For impossible instructions the preferred human recovery strategy is to propose an alternative plan. — [Marge and Rudnicky 2015](https://aclanthology.org/W15-4604/)
- **Unsafe actions as a class of ambiguity [V].** KnowNo's mobile-manipulation tests include unsafe actions (the paper's example instruction is "place the bowl in the microwave") among the cases that should trigger a question. — [arXiv 2307.01928](https://arxiv.org/html/2307.01928v1)
- **The agent's own speech as context [V].** In the Givenness Hierarchy coding used by GH-POWER, any entity mentioned previously in the current dialogue is at least "familiar" and the current speech act and gesture targets are at least "activated", with no distinction by speaker. — [Williams et al. 2016](https://hrilab.tufts.edu/publications/williamsetal16hri.pdf)
- **Out-of-grammar requests are common [V].** About 14% of CraftAssist utterances asked for an action outside the grammar. — [Srinet et al. 2020](https://aclanthology.org/2020.acl-main.427.pdf)

### Inferences
- "Jump into that hole" ending in a walk and a fall is two failures the literature names separately: the action type was silently replaced (jump became move), and no capacity or safety check ran on the pointed spot. Briggs and Scheutz's own demonstration is a robot refusing to walk off an edge until the person explicitly overrides. A rule-based version: before executing, test reachability, line of sight, ammunition, stance and drop height for the resolved place; on failure say the specific reason and, where one exists, offer the nearest feasible alternative ("I can't jump in there. Move to the edge?"); let an explicit confirmation override the safety checks but not the capacity checks.
- If an intent carries a preposition or manner the planner cannot express ("into", "over", "through"), refusing with the reason is better supported than substituting the nearest intent.
- Using the bot's own reports needs one more memory slot: the last thing the bot said, typed as question, report or proposal, with a short lifetime. A report ("I'm out of ammo") makes its topic available so that "go find some" or "then fall back" resolve; a proposal ("I can flank left") makes "yes", "do it" and "go" mean acceptance; a question keeps its existing role. This is the question-under-discussion idea reduced to one slot.

### Gaps
- No controlled study was found on which refusal wording players of action games accept; Briggs and Scheutz propose evaluations but report none.
- No published work was found on a game companion using its own barks as dialogue context.

## 7. Negation, correction and contrast

### Takeaway
A correction of an action is a distinct utterance type that can often be detected from cheap cues (a negative word, a repetition, timing right after the agent starts moving) before it is understood, and the safe first response is to stop. Understanding it is slot replacement against the previous order. Published accuracy exists for classifying fragment types (about 87% weighted F-score with a learned classifier) but not for end-to-end repair of situated commands.

### Cited Findings
- **Raux and Nakano 2010, action corrections in a virtual world [V].** Corrections were 826 of 6,170 dialogue acts (13.4%), ranging from 6.8% to 30.6% across speakers. Three types: commission (the agent did something conflicting), omission (the agent did not react), degree (right action, not enough of it: "More"). Mean time from the operator starting to move to a commission correction 2.3 s (non-corrections 3.8 s); the probability that an utterance is a commission correction falls sharply with time after an action. Exact repetitions: 45.6% of omission, 22.5% of commission and 43.4% of degree corrections, against 6.4% of non-corrections. Lexical cues: "no", "don't" for commission; "more", "keep" for degree; "okay", "yeah" indicate a non-correction. Commission corrections have higher pitch and intensity. The same words ("Turn left") can be an instruction or any kind of correction depending on context. Design implication stated by the authors: on detecting a commission correction the robot "can stop performing its current action even before understanding the details of the correction". — [Raux and Nakano, SIGdial 2010](https://www.cs.utep.edu/nigel/dynamics/raux-sigdial10.pdf)
- **Thierauf et al., "Do this instead" [V-s].** A complete system in a cognitive robot architecture for spoken corrections during task instruction and during execution, tested on navigation-and-retrieval and meal-assembly tasks; corrections fix both the stored semantics and the overt behaviour. No accuracy figure retrieved. — [Thierauf et al., ACM THRI](https://hrilab.tufts.edu/publications/thierauf2023thri)
- **Fragments and "helpful rejection" [V-s].** Fernández, Ginzburg and Lappin classify non-sentential utterances from contextual features of the fragment and its antecedent. The 2005 conference version (14 classes) reports an 87% weighted F-score, "a 25% improvement over a simple rule-based algorithm baseline"; the 2007 journal version reports about 87% on the full range of classes. A helpful rejection is a negative answer that supplies an alternative; after an assertion it corrects a piece of the previous utterance. — [Fernández et al. 2007](https://aclanthology.org/J07-3005/); [Fernández et al. 2005](https://aclanthology.org/2005.sigdial-1.9/); [definition in arXiv 1511.06995](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1511.06995)
- **"The other one" resolved from discourse history [V].** In the SRI multimodal map system "the discourse history provides information for interpreting phrases such as 'No, the other one'"; in the sample session "Price of the other hotel?" returns the price of the previously shown hotel. — [Kehler et al.](https://www.isi.edu/people-hobbs/wp-content/uploads/sites/58/2023/11/multimodal-aaai.pdf)
- **Misunderstanding versus non-understanding [V-s].** The LeCAREbot project notes existing models handle repair after non-understanding but not after misunderstanding, where the operator's repair comes shortly after the wrong action is recognised. — [LeCAREbot](https://www.inf.uni-hamburg.de/en/inst/ab/wtm/research/project-lecarebot.html)
- **Second-best parses are often right [V].** CraftAssist: top-1 exact match 60.74%, top-2 67.7%; the authors point out that an interactive agent can ask and adapt once told of a mistake. — [Srinet et al. 2020](https://aclanthology.org/2020.acl-main.427.pdf)
- **Undo and stop as actions [V].** CraftAssist's grammar has Stop, Resume and Undo ("revert the specified action, if any"). — [Srinet et al. 2020](https://aclanthology.org/2020.acl-main.427.pdf)

### Inferences
- "No, here, on my pin." fits the helpful-rejection pattern: a negative marker plus a fragment with slot content and no new intent. Rule: if the utterance opens with a correction marker ("no", "not there", "I said", "I meant", "wrong") and carries a place or direction but no intent, copy the last order, overwrite only the slots supplied, and re-issue it. When a deictic and an explicit place co-occur ("here, on my pin"), the explicit place is the more specific description and should win, in line with Kehler's "narrow by content first".
- "Not there, here" needs the negated part discarded and the positive part kept; a two-pattern rule ("not X, Y" and "Y, not X") covers the contrast forms. "The other one" needs the previous order's candidate list kept for one turn so that the runner-up referent can be taken.
- Raux and Nakano's timing result gives a cheap prior: an utterance that begins within about two to three seconds of the bot starting a new action and contains a negative word or repeats the last order is far more likely to be a correction than a new order.
- Stopping first is cheap and supported: a "no" at the start of an utterance can halt the current action immediately while the rest is parsed.
- Three-way treatment by type is worth copying: omission (repetition, nothing happened) leads to relaxing defaults and acting; commission (wrong thing) leads to stop plus slot replacement; degree ("more", "closer", "further") leads to adjusting the last order's magnitude.

### Gaps
- No end-to-end accuracy was found for rule-based repair of situated commands; Thierauf et al. report a working system, not a figure.
- The share of fragments in command dialogue and per-class accuracy for corrections in the Fernández et al. study were not retrieved.
- Prosodic cues (raised pitch on commission corrections) are documented but no light, published detector for them was found.

## 8. How much "reasoning" these tasks really need

### Takeaway
Where it has been measured, the large gains come from filling in what was left unsaid, resolving the referent and being allowed to ask, not from deeper planning. Planning depth in short-command settings is shallow by construction, and the remaining errors are dominated by wrong arguments and unreachable targets.

### Cited Findings
- **Implicit actions [V-s].** MARCO: 61% with inference of unstated actions, 28% without (humans 69%). — [MacMahon et al. 2006](https://www.cs.utexas.edu/ftp/qsim/papers/MacMahon-aaai-06.pdf)
- **Permission to ask [V / V-s].** KnowNo: task success 0.38 to 0.74 and 0.51 to 0.76 when the planner may ask. DialFRED: 18.3% to 33.6%. — [arXiv 2307.01928](https://arxiv.org/html/2307.01928v1); [arXiv 2202.13330](https://arxiv.org/abs/2202.13330)
- **Simple tasks need simple resolution [V].** In the multimodal map corpora every reference was resolved by a two-rule decision list and naive recency also did very well, which the authors attribute to task simplicity. — [Kehler et al.](https://www.isi.edu/people-hobbs/wp-content/uploads/sites/58/2023/11/multimodal-aaai.pdf)
- **Error shares in parsers [V].** CraftAssist: 199 of 363 errors have the correct tree and wrong leaves; 125 are span-only. Lang2LTL: recognition and grounding of referring expressions about 98% each, the end-to-end figure 81.83%, the dominant error an unknown template. — [Srinet et al. 2020](https://aclanthology.org/2020.acl-main.427.pdf); [arXiv 2302.11649](https://arxiv.org/html/2302.11649v2)
- **Error shares in embodied agents [V-s].** Grounding or target-not-found about 26–36% of failures, inaccessible object or interaction failure about 16–40%, language processing about 25–33% (ALFRED analyses); about half from visual grounding and 18% from parsing complex sentences (2026 analysis on EB-ALFRED). — [arXiv 2304.03767](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2304.03767); [arXiv 2211.03267](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2211.03267); [arXiv 2604.07774](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.07774)
- **What form-based reference resolution misses [V].** GH-POWER: 10.26% of references needed gesture, 10.60% were regions or parts, against 1.32% lost to salience scoring. — [Williams et al. 2016](https://hrilab.tufts.edu/publications/williamsetal16hri.pdf)
- **Large models are not the planners [V-s].** About 12% average plan success for the best model alone; the working systems pair a translator with a planner or verifier. — [arXiv 2305.15771](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2305.15771v1); [arXiv 2402.01817](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.01817v3)

### Inferences
- For one-line orders to a single bot the "reasoning" the owner asks for decomposes into five rule-sized pieces, each with published support: a referent decision list (section 1), per-intent default chains with a staleness and risk check (section 2), a correction and repetition handler (section 7), a precondition check with a spoken reason (section 6), and a four-field temporal wrapper (section 5). None requires search or a language model; all run in well under a millisecond after the existing classifier.
- A model becomes worth its cost only where the closed lists run out: open-ended conditions ("when it looks clear"), novel place descriptions ("that hole", "behind the red truck") and multi-step goals with no stated steps. The cited numbers suggest that even there a small translator into the same order format, with grammar-constrained output, is the proportionate step, and that accuracy away from the training distribution (49–78% zero-shot for Lang2LTL, 44–63% for NL2TL in a later re-evaluation) is the risk to test for.
- The owner's four test cases map one-to-one onto the measured categories: "Shoot, shoot, shoot" is an unsaid argument plus an omission repetition; "Shoot here / there" is deictic binding at word time; "No, here, on my pin" is a helpful rejection with slot replacement; "jump into that hole" is action-type substitution plus a missing safety precondition. None is a planning failure.

### Gaps
- No study was found that splits instruction-following errors into "fixed by reference and defaults" versus "fixed by deeper planning" for short commands in a real-time game; the claim above is assembled from adjacent settings (route instructions, household robots, map interfaces, Minecraft).
- Benchmarks rarely count "wrong default" as its own category, because instructions are usually fully specified; the size of that category for spoken game commands is unmeasured.
- The web-search budget shared across this research run was exhausted before the last round: HuRIC, radio and fire-command formats, behaviour-tree generation, CVDN, IGLU, Voyager and Talk the Walk, the Menghi coverage figure and several classic references marked [R] could not be checked and should be verified before being quoted with numbers.
