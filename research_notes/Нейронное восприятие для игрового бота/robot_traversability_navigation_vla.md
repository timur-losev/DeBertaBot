# Robot perception for geometry, traversability and navigation, and "robot brain" foundation models (state as of October 2026)

Reader context: a game-AI team (Unreal Engine 5.8, exact geometry available from the engine, CPU-only runtime; the current intent classifier costs about 40 ms on 3 cores of an Intel i9-10900K; ONNX Runtime available). "Older work" below means published before 2024. Research date: 2026-10-10. Numbers are quoted from the primary source linked on the same line unless marked otherwise.

## 1. How do legged and wheeled robots detect and represent pits, negative obstacles, stairs, steps, slopes, cliffs and thin obstacles?

### Takeaway
Real robots almost never label "pit / stair / ledge" explicitly. They build a robot-centric 2.5D elevation map (or a 3D TSDF/ESDF voxel map), derive per-cell layers (slope, roughness, step height, "no-step", signed distance to obstacle, learned traversability) and hand those layers to a planner or a locomotion policy. Almost all of the heavy compute (GPU, Jetson) is spent reconstructing geometry from noisy sensors, which a game engine provides for free; the part that transfers is the representation (layered local grids, height samples around the feet, signed distance fields) and the small networks that consume it.

### Cited Findings

Classical geometric pipelines

- `grid_map` (ANYbotics): C++ library for "universal 2.5-dimensional grid mapping with support for any number of layers"; BSD 3-Clause; the core package depends only on Eigen (CPU); ROS 1 on `master`, ROS 2 branches humble/iron/jazzy/rolling. Terrain features are produced by filter chains: `NormalVectorsFilter`, slope as `acos(normal_vectors_z)`, roughness as `abs(elevation - elevation_smooth)`, traversability as a weighted sum of such layers. Older work, still maintained. — [grid_map GitHub](https://github.com/ANYbotics/grid_map)
- `elevation_mapping_cupy` (ETH RSL / leggedrobotics): "Elevation Mapping on GPU"; MIT license; takes multi-modal images and multi-modal point clouds; plugins post-process the map into new layers; includes a "learning-based traversability filter" that assesses terrain "using local geometry". GPU is required; no CPU fallback is described. — [elevation_mapping_cupy GitHub](https://github.com/leggedrobotics/elevation_mapping_cupy)
- The underlying paper (Miki et al., 2022, older work) lists the layers: elevation with variance (Kalman-style update), an upper-bound layer from ray casting for unobserved cells, a traversability layer from a small CNN, normals, a minimum-filter inpainting layer for occluded cells, smoothing filters, and plane segmentation that returns polygons. Extra features: height drift compensation, visibility cleanup by ray casting, overlap clearance for multi-floor scenes, and an exclusion area against ceilings/overhangs. — [arXiv 2204.12876](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2204.12876)
- Compute for that pipeline: 10 x 10 m map at 4 cm resolution; 6.857 ms total per update for 43,017 points on a Jetson Xavier, of which the traversability CNN is 4.102 ms; map update at 49.4 Hz (6,276 filtered RealSense points), 16.1 Hz (407,040 raw RealSense points) and 19.99 Hz (43,074 Bpearl LiDAR points) on Jetson Xavier. Sensors on ANYmal: two Robosense Bpearl LiDARs or four Intel RealSense cameras. — [arXiv 2204.12876](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2204.12876)
- What ANYmal's locomotion policy actually reads from that map (Miki et al., Science Robotics 2022, older work): 52 height samples per foot on circles of 6/8/10/12/16 points at radii 0.08/0.16/0.26/0.36/0.48 m, i.e. 208 inputs for four feet; map built at 20 Hz; policy at 50 Hz; cells with no data are filled with a random value; a 2-layer GRU belief encoder (50 hidden units each) with an attention gate decides how much exteroception to trust, and the controller degrades to proprioceptive walking when the map is wrong (it still climbed stairs with sensors covered). — [arXiv 2201.08117](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2201.08117)
- `nvblox` (NVIDIA): "a GPU-accelerated TSDF and ESDF library for robots equipped with RGB-D cameras"; uses CUDA. — [nvblox GitHub](https://github.com/nvidia-isaac/nvblox). The Isaac ROS documentation says it "builds a voxel-based map from depth-images and/or 3D LiDAR scans", outputs a real-time mesh and a cost map for path planning, and has Static, People Segmentation, People Detection and Dynamics modes; supported configurations include up to 4 RealSense cameras at 30 Hz on Jetson Orin 64GB and 1 static camera on Orin Nano 8GB. — [Isaac ROS nvblox docs](https://nvidia-isaac-ros.github.io/concepts/scene_reconstruction/nvblox/index.html)
- NVIDIA Isaac Perceptor: nvblox is the CUDA-accelerated 3D reconstruction core and "can identify obstacles up to five meters away to provide a 2D costmap"; multi-camera support of up to three HAWK stereo cameras gives about 270 degrees field of view. — [NVIDIA developer blog](https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/create-design-and-deploy-robotics-applications-using-new-nvidia-isaac-foundation-models-and-workflows). The Nav2 tutorial describes vision-only (lidar-free) navigation with Isaac Perceptor on Jetson Orin or Thor, using cuVSLAM for odometry/localization. — [Open Robotics Discourse](https://discourse.openrobotics.org/t/new-nav2-tutorial-lidar-free-navigation-using-nvidia-isaac-perceptor-ros/51411)
- Voxblox (ETH ASL, older work): TSDF and ESDF ("building Euclidean Signed Distance Fields directly from TSDFs"), meshing; BSD-3-Clause; "CPU-only, can be run single-threaded or multi-threaded for some integrators". — [voxblox GitHub](https://github.com/ethz-asl/voxblox)
- OctoMap (older work): "An Efficient Probabilistic 3D Mapping Framework Based on Octrees"; `octomap` is under the New BSD License, `octovis` and related libraries are GPL. — [OctoMap GitHub](https://github.com/OctoMap/octomap)

Boston Dynamics Spot (production reference)

- Spot's local grid service exposes five robot-centric grids: "terrain" (estimated z-height per cell), "terrain_valid", "intensity", "no_step" (boolean per cell: whether the robot may step there, given body and nearby terrain/obstacles) and "obstacle_distance" (signed distance to the nearest obstacle, positive outside, negative inside; walls, large obstacles and platforms higher than 40 cm count as obstacles). The grids are rebuilt each instant and are not accumulated over time. — [Spot SDK: robot services](https://dev.bostondynamics.com/docs/concepts/robot_services)
- Stairs: Spot detects stairs in its path by default and adjusts gait and body pitch to the slope. — [Boston Dynamics support: How Spot Moves](https://support.bostondynamics.com/s/article/How-Spot-Moves-49929). The SDK exposes a stairs mode with On / Auto / Off values (the old boolean stair hint is deprecated). — [Spot SDK robot command reference](https://dev.bostondynamics.com/python/bosdyn-client/src/bosdyn/client/robot_command). GraphNav map edges can carry annotations such as stairs mode and speed restrictions that "control how the robot should move between them". — [Spot SDK: components of autonomous navigation](https://dev.bostondynamics.com/docs/concepts/autonomy/components_of_autonomous_navigation)
- Since Release 2.0 (2020, older work) Spot fits a regular-spacing stair model to its map and sensor data to choose footholds. — [IEEE Spectrum](https://spectrum.ieee.org/spots-new-upgrades-bring-enhanced-autonomy-payload-integration-and-stair-smarts). Boston Dynamics still tells operators that stairs are hazardous and that staircases should be checked against nominal operating dimensions. — [Navigate Spot on Stairs](https://support.bostondynamics.com/s/article/Navigate-Spot-on-Stairs-49953); [Prepare Your Site for Spot Operation](https://support.bostondynamics.com/s/article/Prepare-Your-Site-for-Spot-Operation-49914)

Negative obstacles, cliffs, drop-offs

- A range sensor sees a drop-off as a region with no returns; "the blind area is the reason why the negative obstacles cannot be detected by the positive obstacles algorithm". One fix is to artificially fill in the blind spot by recursively expanding the map so the elevation jump becomes visible, at the cost that "false obstacles could appear" where noise creates holes. — [arXiv 2403.13431](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2403.13431)
- DARPA SubT winner Team CERBERUS (2022, older work) used a robot-centric elevation map with ray-cast clearing, protecting currently observed cells so thin structures are not erased. — [arXiv 2207.04914](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2207.04914)
- Team CSIRO Data61 used GridMap traversability mapping alongside an occupancy grid with "virtual surfaces" to explore safely amid negative obstacles. — [Frontiers in Robotics and AI review](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/robotics-and-ai/articles/10.3389/frobt.2023.1249586/pdf)
- A SubT planner for mixed Spot/wheeled teams labels OctoMap voxels with terrain classes, including a "stair" label used by legged robots and a "rough" label used by wheeled ones (2023, older work). — [arXiv 2301.00771](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2301.00771)

Stairs and multi-level surfaces (2025 to 2026)

- Height maps fail on open-tread stairs because several surfaces share one (x, y); a GPU voxel-map multi-plane segmentation method running on Jetson AGX Orin addresses this for a wheel-legged robot and a Unitree Go2. — [arXiv 2510.01592](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.01592)
- PolygMap fuses LiDAR, RGB-D and IMU into a polygonal stair-plane map for humanoid footstep planning, 20 to 30 Hz whole-body planning output on an NVIDIA Orin. — [arXiv 2510.12346](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.12346)
- A March 2026 humanoid stair paper uses a robot-centric LiDAR elevation map for omnidirectional coverage (forward depth cameras leave blind zones) and adds a dense penalty for foot placements near edges. — [arXiv 2603.07928](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.07928)

Learned traversability

- Wild Visual Navigation (ETH RSL / Oxford): online self-supervised visual traversability; MIT-licensed code; inputs are RGB images plus robot velocity estimates and commanded velocity; features from DINO (ViT-small), DINOv2 or STEGO; requires a CUDA GPU; ROS 1 Noetic. — [wild_visual_navigation GitHub](https://github.com/leggedrobotics/wild_visual_navigation)
- WVN details: images resized to 224 x 224; the learned part is only a two-layer MLP (256 and 32 units); supervision is traction, measured as the error between commanded and estimated planar velocity; it bootstraps in under 5 minutes of in-field demonstration; runs onboard a Jetson Orin AGX on ANYmal C/D; the prediction is ray-cast onto a robot-centric 2.5D elevation map that serves as a cost map for a reactive local planner. — [arXiv 2404.07110](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.07110)
- RoadRunner (JPL / ETH, 2024, accepted in IEEE T-FR): predicts traversability cost and an elevation map in bird's-eye view directly from camera and LiDAR; trained self-supervised with labels generated in hindsight by an existing traversability stack; latency drops about 4x, from 500 ms to 140 ms. — [arXiv 2402.19341](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.19341)
- STEPP (ICRA 2025): learns traversability from demonstrations of human walking, tested on ANYmal. — [arXiv 2501.17594](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.17594v1)
- The only dedicated survey found is from 2022 (older work): "A Survey of Traversability Estimation for Mobile Robots". — [arXiv 2204.10883](https://arxiv.org/abs/2204.10883v1)

### Inferences
- Directly transferable to the game bot (cheap on CPU, no learning needed): (a) a robot-centric layered local grid in the style of Spot's `terrain` / `no_step` / `obstacle_distance` grids or grid_map layers, filled from engine line traces or a heightfield query instead of sensors; (b) per-cell features slope = acos(normal.z), roughness = |h - smooth(h)|, step height, and a signed distance to obstacles; (c) Spot's rule "anything higher than 40 cm is an obstacle, below that it is terrain" as the pattern for splitting steps from walls by the agent's step height; (d) the "unknown or occluded cell near an edge is suspect, not free" rule for drop-offs, which in a game becomes "no ground hit within N cm below the expected surface = pit/ledge".
- The ANYmal foot-centred sampling pattern (52 height samples per foot on 5 rings out to 0.48 m) is a ready-made, compact observation vector for a small learned model: in the engine it is a few hundred downward traces, and unlike on the robot it is noise-free, so the belief encoder / attention gate that copes with bad maps is unnecessary.
- Not transferable: everything whose job is to recover geometry from sensors (TSDF fusion in nvblox/Voxblox, OctoMap occupancy, drift compensation, visibility cleanup, cuVSLAM, stereo depth). The engine already has ground-truth collision geometry and a navmesh; adding these would only re-derive it with noise.
- Thin obstacles such as poles are handled in robot stacks by the 3D side (ESDF / obstacle-distance grid, ray-cast clearing that protects observed thin structures), not by the 2.5D elevation layer. For the bot the equivalent is a capsule/sphere sweep or a distance-field query rather than a height grid.
- The open-tread-stairs finding (height maps break when several surfaces share one (x, y)) is a warning for any 2.5D grid in a game with multi-storey levels, overhangs or bridges: use per-floor grids or 3D queries.
- WVN's visual-feature step (DINO on GPU) solves a problem the game does not have: the engine can report the physical surface material directly. What remains useful is the idea of self-supervising a traversability score from the agent's own velocity-tracking error.

### Gaps
- nvblox license: the repository page did not show the license name and the LICENSE file could not be fetched (HTTP 404/503). It is Apache-2.0 to the best of my recollection, but this was not verified in this session.
- The Isaac Perceptor documentation page returned HTTP 404, so the exact required compute, release version and license could not be confirmed from the primary docs; the facts above come from an NVIDIA blog post and the Nav2 announcement.
- The Boston Dynamics support articles are JavaScript-rendered and could not be opened; the Spot stairs statements come from search-result excerpts of those articles. I found no official statement in this session on descent orientation (rear-first) or on how Spot treats cliffs/ledges; grid cell sizes are "parameterized" and no default resolution was found.
- No quantitative benchmark for negative-obstacle detection on legged robots was found. No 2025 or 2026 survey dedicated to traversability estimation was found; the 2022 survey is the latest confirmed.
- No published CPU-only timing was found for any of the learned traversability models (the elevation_mapping_cupy traversability CNN is 4.1 ms on a Jetson Xavier GPU; its parameter count is not stated).
- RoadRunner M&M (follow-up) and the vehicle platform were not confirmed from a primary source in this session.

## 2. Which navigation foundation models exist, what do they take and output, how large are they, and can any run on CPU?

### Takeaway
There are two families: small image-goal policies from Berkeley (GNM 8.7M, ViNT 31M, NoMaD 19M parameters; MIT; 85 x 64 pixel inputs; waypoints out), which are small enough for CPU inference, and 7 to 8 billion parameter video-language navigation models (Uni-NaVid, NaVILA, NavFoM, OmniVLA) that need a workstation GPU and run at about 1 to 5 Hz. All of them exist to turn camera pixels into local waypoints; none consumes or produces explicit geometry, so with a navmesh available their function is redundant for the game bot.

### Cited Findings

Small goal-image policies (Berkeley)

- GNM (2022, older work): inputs are the current and goal RGB images at 85 x 64 pixels plus the past 5 observations; two MobileNetv2 encoders; outputs 5 future normalized waypoints (x, y, yaw) and a normalized temporal distance to the goal; trained on over 60 hours from 6 robots. — [arXiv 2210.03370](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2210.03370). GNM has 8.7M trainable parameters. — [ViNT paper, arXiv 2306.14846](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2306.14846)
- ViNT (2023, older work): about 31M parameters; EfficientNet-B0 encoders plus a 4-layer, 4-head, 512-dim transformer; 85 x 64 RGB with 5 past frames; outputs temporal distance and 5 future relative waypoints; runs at 4 Hz with a PD controller tracking waypoints; long-range navigation needs a topological graph plus a 318M-parameter image diffusion model that proposes subgoals (200 DDIM steps). — [arXiv 2306.14846](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2306.14846)
- NoMaD (October 2023, older work): 19M parameters; two EfficientNet-B0 encoders, 4-layer transformer, and a 1D conditional U-Net (15 conv layers) diffusion action head with 10 denoising steps; goal masking (p = 0.5 in training) gives one model for goal-directed navigation and undirected exploration; trained on GNM + SACSoN, over 100 hours; the paper names NVIDIA Jetson Orin as the class of onboard computer it fits. — [arXiv 2310.07896](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2310.07896)
- The shared repository for GNM / ViNT / NoMaD is MIT-licensed; deployment example is a LoCoBot with a wide-angle RGB camera and a Jetson Orin Nano; navigation follows a topological map built from a recorded trajectory; the model publishes waypoints and a separate PD controller turns them into velocities. — [visualnav-transformer GitHub](https://github.com/robodhruv/visualnav-transformer)

Large video-language navigation models

- Uni-NaVid: Vicuna-7B (v1.5) LLM with an EVA-CLIP vision encoder; input is video plus a language instruction; about 5 Hz on a single A100 GPU with online token merging; tasks: vision-language navigation, object-goal, EQA and human following; code is MIT; released checkpoint `uninavid-7b-full-224-video-fps-1-grid-2`; the weights license is not stated on the repo. — [Uni-NaVid GitHub](https://github.com/jzhzhang/Uni-NaVid). A community fine-tune is tagged with the llama2 license because the weights derive from Vicuna / Llama 2. — [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/Eku127/uninavid-satnav-scratch-1ep-lr1e-5)
- NaVILA (December 2024): an 8B-parameter VILA-based VLA; single RGB camera, current frame plus 8 history frames in real-world runs; output is mid-level language actions such as "moving forward 75cm" or turns, parsed by a regex; about 1 FPS on a single RTX 4090; W4A16 quantization cut GPU memory from 18.5 GB to 8.6 GB and latency from 594.58 ms to 367.80 ms per query; a separate RL locomotion policy consumes a 2.5D height map built from a Unitree L1 LiDAR and handles obstacle avoidance; 88% real-world success over 25 instructions; robots: Unitree Go2, Booster T1 (H1 in simulation). — [arXiv 2412.04453](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.04453)
- NaVILA code is Apache-2.0; checkpoints `a8cheng/navila-llama3-8b-8f` and `a8cheng/navila-siglip-llama3-8b-v1.5-pretrain` are on Hugging Face; a license for the weights is not stated on the repo page. — [NaVILA GitHub](https://github.com/AnjieCheng/NaVILA)
- NavFoM, "Embodied Navigation Foundation Model" (arXiv September 2025, ICLR 2026; Peking University / Galbot and collaborators): Qwen2-7B LLM with DINOv2 and SigLIP encoders; inputs are egocentric RGB video from one to eight cameras plus text, no depth or odometry; a three-layer MLP head outputs 8 waypoints ((x, y, theta), or (x, y, z, theta) for UAVs); at most 0.5 s per trajectory with a 1600-token budget on an RTX 4090; the model runs on a remote server and a local planner follows the trajectory; trained on 12.7M samples (8.02M navigation). — [arXiv 2509.12129](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.12129v2); [ICLR 2026 proceedings](https://proceedings.iclr.cc/paper_files/paper/2026/hash/ceb45c48e29e7f49b0b47edb98e43691-Abstract-Conference.html)
- OmniVLA (September 2025; repo last pushed March 2026): navigation VLA built on OpenVLA checkpoints (`openvla/openvla-7b`) and the OpenVLA-OFT codebase; goal modalities are language, goal images and GPS pose; outputs a trajectory / velocity commands; repo carries an MIT license badge; checkpoints on Hugging Face include `NHirose/omnivla-original` and a lighter `NHirose/omnivla-edge`; training used 8 H100 GPUs. — [OmniVLA GitHub](https://github.com/NHirose/OmniVLA)
- NavDP (arXiv May 2025, listed as ICRA 2026): sim-to-real navigation diffusion policy; RGB-D input; no-goal, point-goal and image-goal modes; trained without real robot data; code is CC BY-NC-SA 4.0 (non-commercial); checkpoints are not openly downloadable (Google form). — [NavDP GitHub](https://github.com/InternRobotics/NavDP)
- NVIDIA COMPASS (arXiv February 2025): cross-embodiment mobility policy built from the pretrained X-Mobility policy by imitation learning, residual RL in Isaac Lab and distillation. — [arXiv 2502.16372](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2502.16372)

### Inferences
- CPU feasibility (my estimate, not a published measurement): GNM (8.7M), NoMaD (19M) and ViNT (31M) use MobileNetv2 / EfficientNet-B0 encoders on 85 x 64 images, which is far below the cost of the bot's current 40 ms classifier per forward pass; NoMaD adds 10 passes of a small 1D U-Net. They are plausibly exportable to ONNX and runnable on CPU within the budget, but nobody publishes CPU numbers and it must be measured.
- Even so, they are a poor fit: their input is a rendered first-person image and a goal image, and their output is a short waypoint list that a navmesh query produces exactly and for free. Rendering a camera view per bot on the server would cost far more than the model. They also depend on a topological graph of images for anything beyond local motion.
- The 7 to 8B models (Uni-NaVid, NaVILA, NavFoM, OmniVLA) are excluded at runtime: 0.2 to 1 s per step on an RTX 4090 or A100, 8 to 18 GB of GPU memory, and Llama 2 / Vicuna / Qwen-derived weights whose licenses need review for a commercial game.
- The reusable idea is the interface, not the weights: NaVILA's split (a slow language model emits mid-level commands such as "move forward 75 cm" / "turn left 30 degrees"; a fast geometry-aware controller executes them and avoids obstacles) is the same shape as "intent classifier, then engine-side planner" and confirms that language should stop at a small command vocabulary. NavFoM's "8 waypoints, then a local planner" is the same pattern.
- NoMaD's goal masking (one network for "go to goal" and "explore") is a training trick that could carry over to a learned bot movement policy, if one is ever trained on engine-side observations.

### Gaps
- No statement on public release or license of NavFoM weights was found in the paper text read (first 100k characters) or on arXiv; unverified as of October 2026.
- Parameter counts are not published for OmniVLA-edge, NavDP, X-Mobility or COMPASS; licenses for X-Mobility / COMPASS were not found.
- Weight licenses for the official Uni-NaVid and NaVILA checkpoints are not stated on their repositories; both inherit from Llama-family or Vicuna base models, so "MIT" / "Apache-2.0" applies to code only.
- No CPU-only inference measurement was found for any navigation foundation model.
- I found no navigation foundation model newer than NavFoM (ICLR 2026) and NavDP (ICRA 2026) that is clearly established; other 2026 preprints may exist and were not surveyed exhaustively.

## 3. Which VLA and embodied-reasoning models exist as of late 2026, and are any practical for a CPU-only game bot?

### Takeaway
The current generation is π0.7 (about 5B parameters, April 2026), GR00T N1.7 (3B, 2026), Figure Helix 02 (January 2026), Gemini Robotics ER 2 (July 2026, API only) and the small open SmolVLA (450M, Apache-2.0). Every one of them needs a GPU (8 to 16 GB or more) and measures inference in tens to hundreds of milliseconds on an RTX 4090 / H100 class card; none is practical inside a CPU-only game loop. Their realistic role is offline: labelling, data generation, design-time reasoning via API.

### Cited Findings

Open-weight VLAs

- OpenVLA (June 2024): 7B parameters (SigLIP + DINOv2 encoders of about 600M, Llama 2 7B backbone); 224 x 224 image plus instruction in, 7-DoF action as 7 tokens of 256 bins out; about 6 Hz on one RTX 4090 in bfloat16; 15 GB memory (16.8 GB in the quantization table), 10.2 GB at int8 (1.2 Hz on an A5000), 7.0 GB at int4 (3 Hz on an A5000); trained on 970k trajectories with 64 A100 GPUs for 14 days. — [arXiv 2406.09246](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.09246)
- OpenVLA licensing: code is MIT, but the released models derive from Llama 2 and are "subject to the Llama Community License"; OpenVLA-OFT gives 25 to 50x faster inference than vanilla OpenVLA fine-tuning. — [OpenVLA GitHub](https://github.com/openvla/openvla)
- Octo (2024): MIT license; Octo-Small 27M parameters at about 17 iterations/s and Octo-Base 93M at about 13 iterations/s on a single RTX 4090; RGB views plus language or goal image in, chunks of 4 actions out; trained on about 800k Open X-Embodiment trajectories. — [Octo GitHub](https://github.com/octo-models/octo)
- π0 (Physical Intelligence, October 2024): 3.3B parameters (about 3B PaliGemma VLM plus a 300M action expert); 50-step action chunks at up to 50 Hz; inference 73 ms on-board on an RTX 4090 with 3 cameras (14 ms image encoders, 32 ms observation pass, 27 ms for 10 flow steps); trained on over 10,000 hours of robot data. — [arXiv 2410.24164](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.24164)
- `openpi` repository: Apache-2.0 (plus a `LICENSE_GEMMA.txt` file); provides π0, π0-FAST and π0.5 base checkpoints (π0.5 and PyTorch support added September 2025); needs an NVIDIA GPU with more than 8 GB for inference, more than 22.5 GB for LoRA fine-tuning and more than 70 GB for full fine-tuning; π0.6 and π0.7 are not in the repository. — [openpi GitHub](https://github.com/Physical-Intelligence/openpi)
- π0.7 (blog dated April 16, 2026): Gemma 3 4B backbone (including a 400M vision encoder) plus an 860M flow-matching action expert, about 5B in total; inputs are up to four 448 x 448 camera views with up to six history frames each, proprioception, subtask language, episode metadata (speed, quality 1 to 5, mistake flag), control mode and up to three subgoal images generated at runtime by a world model initialized from the 14B BAGEL model; outputs 50-step chunks at 50 Hz with 5 denoising steps and real-time chunking; matches the RL-trained π*0.6 specialists on the reported tasks. The paper does not state that weights or code are released. — [arXiv 2604.15483](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.15483)
- GR00T N1 (NVIDIA, March 2025): 2.2B parameters (1.34B in the Eagle-2 VLM); dual system with the VLM at 10 Hz on an L40 and a diffusion transformer producing actions at 120 Hz; 63.9 ms to sample a 16-action chunk on an L40 in bf16. — [arXiv 2503.14734](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.14734)
- GR00T N1.7 (current release; early access in 2026): `nvidia/GR00T-N1.7-3B`, about 3B parameters; Cosmos-Reason2-2B backbone (Qwen3-VL architecture) replacing Eagle; flow-matching DiT action head with 16 layers and action horizon 40; inputs are RGB frames, proprioceptive state, language and embodiment ID; inference needs one GPU with 16 GB+ VRAM, fine-tuning 40 GB+; TensorRT and ONNX export supported. — [Isaac-GR00T GitHub](https://github.com/NVIDIA/Isaac-GR00T)
- GR00T N1.7 measured latency (4 denoising steps, 1 camera, end to end): 85.8 ms on H100 in PyTorch eager; 27.9 ms on H100 with full TensorRT; 38.4 ms on L40; 93.8 ms on Jetson AGX Thor; 98.6 ms on DGX Spark; 216.5 ms on Jetson Orin (TensorRT for the DiT only). The model card states the license is the "NVIDIA Open Model License Agreement" and that the model is "ready for commercial/non-commercial use". — [Hugging Face model card](https://huggingface.co/nvidia/GR00T-N1.7-3B)
- License conflict: the GitHub README says code is Apache 2.0 and weights are under the NVIDIA Open Model License, and also says "GR00T N1.7 is fully commercially licensable under Apache 2.0". — [Isaac-GR00T GitHub](https://github.com/NVIDIA/Isaac-GR00T); contradicted by [Hugging Face model card](https://huggingface.co/nvidia/GR00T-N1.7-3B)
- SmolVLA (Hugging Face LeRobot, June 2025): about 450M parameters (model card lists 0.5B), of which about 100M is the action expert; SmolVLM-2 backbone using only the first 16 LLM layers, 64 visual tokens per frame, 10 flow-matching steps; pretrained on 481 community datasets (22.9K episodes, 10.6M frames); about 6x less memory than π0; asynchronous inference finished tasks about 30% faster (9.7 s vs 13.75 s). — [arXiv 2506.01844](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.01844). License Apache-2.0; inputs are multi-view images, proprioceptive state and an optional instruction; outputs continuous actions. — [Hugging Face model card](https://huggingface.co/lerobot/smolvla_base). Marketing claim: "Small enough to run on CPU, train on a single consumer GPU, or even a MacBook". — [Hugging Face blog](https://huggingface.co/blog/smolvla)

Closed or API-only

- Figure Helix (original): System 2 is a 7B-parameter open-weight VLM at 7 to 9 Hz, System 1 is an 80M-parameter visuomotor policy at 200 Hz, each on its own onboard low-power embedded GPU; 35-DoF upper-body action space; about 500 hours of teleoperation data; no license or release terms. — [Figure](https://www.figure.ai/news/helix)
- Figure Helix 02 (January 27, 2026): adds System 0, a 10M-parameter whole-body controller at 1 kHz that replaces 109,504 lines of hand-engineered C++, trained on over 1,000 hours of retargeted human motion and more than 200,000 parallel simulation environments; S1 at 200 Hz now drives the whole body from head cameras, palm cameras, fingertip tactile sensors and proprioception; no availability or licensing terms. — [Figure](https://www.figure.ai/news/helix-02)
- Gemini Robotics 1.5 (September 25, 2025): the VLA "thinks before acting"; Gemini Robotics-ER 1.5 acts as a high-level planner that calls the VLA and other tools; ER 1.5 was released through the Gemini API, the VLA only to select partners. — [Google DeepMind blog](https://deepmind.google/blog/gemini-robotics-15-brings-ai-agents-into-the-physical-world/)
- Gemini Robotics-ER 1.6 was released April 14, 2026. — [Google DeepMind blog](https://deepmind.google/blog/gemini-robotics-er-1-6). It is deprecated as of August 31, 2026; the current endpoints are `gemini-robotics-er-2-preview` and `gemini-robotics-er-2-streaming-preview` (Live API), in preview; inputs are text, images, video and audio; outputs are text including 2D points as normalized [y, x] on a 0 to 1000 scale, bounding boxes, trajectories, task plans and function calls; higher thinking levels increase latency. — [Gemini API robotics docs](https://ai.google.dev/gemini-api/docs/robotics-overview)
- Gemini Robotics ER 2 model card (published July 30, 2026): based on Gemini 3.5 Flash; context up to 128k, output up to 64K tokens; available in Google AI Studio, the Gemini API and (private preview) Gemini Enterprise Agent Platform under the Gemini API Additional Terms of Service; no open license; not to be used for safety-critical work; Gemini Robotics On-Device 2 is named as a related model. — [Google DeepMind model card](https://deepmind.google/models/model-cards/gemini-robotics-er-2/)
- Gemini Robotics On-Device (June 24, 2025): a VLA optimized to run locally on the robot; private preview through a trusted-tester program; adapts with 50 to 100 demonstrations; no parameter count published. — [Google DeepMind](https://deepmind.google/models/gemini-robotics/on-device/)

### Inferences
- None of these models fits a CPU-only real-time budget. The smallest published GPU latencies are 27.9 ms (GR00T N1.7, H100 + TensorRT) and 73 ms (π0, RTX 4090) for 3B-class models; a CPU is typically one to two orders of magnitude slower than those GPUs for transformer inference, which puts them at seconds per step. SmolVLA at 450M with 10 flow steps is the only candidate advertised for CPU, but no CPU latency is published; expect hundreds of milliseconds to seconds, to be measured before any plan depends on it.
- More fundamentally, a VLA's job is "camera pixels + joint state to joint-level action chunks". A game bot has neither cameras nor motors to learn: the engine gives exact state and the character controller executes high-level moves. The VLA output space (7 to 132-dimensional continuous robot actions) does not map onto bot actions.
- What is reusable is the architecture pattern and the offline role: (a) Gemini Robotics ER 2 or any general VLM via API as a design-time or build-time tool (auto-labelling screenshots or level regions, generating training utterances and expected plans, checking task success in recorded clips); the Gemini terms apply and it is network-bound, so it cannot sit in the shipped runtime; (b) the fast/slow split below (section 5).
- Licensing for a commercial game: clean permissive options are SmolVLA (Apache-2.0), Octo (MIT) and the openpi code (Apache-2.0, with a Gemma licence file for the PaliGemma-derived weights). OpenVLA weights carry the Llama 2 Community License. GR00T N1.7 weights are under the NVIDIA Open Model License, with contradictory "Apache 2.0" wording in the README; legal review is needed before any use. π0.7, Helix and the Gemini Robotics VLAs are not available as weights.

### Gaps
- π0.7 inference hardware and latency are in an appendix that was not read; whether Physical Intelligence released π0.7 or π0.6 weights was not confirmed (they are absent from openpi). One secondary source describes π0.7 as 7B; the paper text read says about 5B (4B + 860M), which I treat as authoritative. The Physical Intelligence blog itself returned HTTP 429 and was not read.
- π0.5 parameter count was not confirmed in this session (openpi only gives the more-than-8 GB inference requirement).
- No published CPU latency exists for SmolVLA, Octo or any other VLA in the sources read.
- A "GR00T N2" was searched for and not found; the latest confirmed NVIDIA model is GR00T N1.7. The exact dates of the N1.7 announcement (GTC, March 2026) and early-access release (April 17, 2026) come from a secondary wiki, not from NVIDIA.
- The Gemini Robotics 2 family reportedly has three sub-models of which only ER 2 is public; this comes from secondary coverage. Parameter counts for all Gemini Robotics models are unpublished. Pricing was not verified from the official pricing page.
- Other proprietary "robot brain" efforts (for example from 1X, Skild AI, Generalist, Tesla) were not researched.

## 4. Is there published work on affordance or "semantic terrain" perception (stairs, doors, ledges, gaps), and what representation feeds the policy?

### Takeaway
In legged parkour and perceptive locomotion the policy is fed raw geometry, not labels: a privileged "teacher" trained in simulation reads exact terrain heights ("scandots" / height scans / obstacle parameters), and a deployable "student" reads a small egocentric depth image (48 x 64 to 58 x 87 pixels) or a reconstructed height map / voxel grid. Explicit semantic labels appear only as privileged training inputs (an obstacle-category one-hot), as the output of a high-level policy (a skill index), or as human annotations on a map (Spot's stairs edges). A game bot sits in the teacher's position, with exact geometry, so the teacher-side representations are the ones to copy.

### Cited Findings

- Robot Parkour Learning (Zhuang et al., 2023, older work): five skills (climb, leap, crawl, tilt through a slit, run); real-world limits: climb 0.40 m (1.53x robot height), leap 0.60 m (1.5x robot length), crawl under 0.20 m, squeeze through a 0.28 m slit; privileged simulation-only inputs are distance to the obstacle ahead, obstacle height and width, and a 4-dimensional one-hot obstacle category; the deployed policy sees a 48 x 64 egocentric depth image (RealSense D435), 29-dim proprioception and the previous action; network is a CNN (16/32/32 channels, 128-dim embedding), a 256-unit GRU and an MLP [512, 256, 128]; outputs 12 joint position targets; runs at 50 Hz on a Jetson NX with depth refreshed at 10 Hz; five specialist policies are distilled into one with DAgger; training uses "soft" penetrable obstacles first, then hard collisions. — [arXiv 2309.05665](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2309.05665)
- Extreme Parkour (Cheng et al., 2023, older work): the phase-1 teacher takes scandots (terrain height points), target heading, a walking flag and commanded speed; the deployed student takes a single front depth camera (RealSense D435, 10 +/- 2 Hz, cropped and downsampled to 58 x 87) through a conv-GRU encoder and also predicts its own heading direction from depth; Unitree A1 with a Jetson NX running the depth backbone at 10 Hz and the policy at 50 Hz; achieves a 0.5 m high jump (2x hip height), a 0.8 m long jump and 37 degree tilted ramps; trained on one RTX 3090 in under 20 hours. — [arXiv 2309.14341](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2309.14341); [project page](https://extreme-parkour.github.io/)
- ANYmal Parkour (Hoeller et al., 2023, older work): three learned modules. Perception: point clouds from six RealSense depth cameras and a Velodyne Puck LiDAR go into a dense 3D conv encoder-decoder that outputs per-voxel occupancy probability and centroid at two resolutions (12.5 cm voxels over 4 m, refined to 6.25 cm over 2 m) plus a latent "scene belief"; runs on a Jetson Orin. Navigation (5 Hz): from the latent and the goal it outputs a categorical skill index plus a local target position, remaining time and target heading. Locomotion (50 Hz): five skills (walk, jump, climb up, climb down, crouch). Capabilities: gaps up to 1 m, boxes up to 1 m, 0.4 m crouch passages, steps to 0.25 m, slopes to 40 degrees, up to 2 m/s. — [arXiv 2306.14874](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2306.14874)
- ANYmal perceptive locomotion (Miki et al., 2022, older work): 208 height samples around the feet from a 2.5D elevation map, teacher trained on noiseless terrain and student trained with injected height noise (60% nominal, 30% large offset, 10% large noise). — [arXiv 2201.08117](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2201.08117)
- NaVILA's locomotion policy (2024): a 2.5D height map from LiDAR (0.06 m voxels in simulation; lowest value per voxel, max-filtered over the last 5 scans) plus proprioception; the critic gets privileged terrain scans. — [arXiv 2412.04453](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.04453)
- "Hiking in the Wild" (January 2026): single-stage end-to-end RL from raw depth and proprioception to joint actions on a full-size humanoid, up to 2.5 m/s; adds Terrain Edge Detection with Foot Volume Points to stop the feet slipping off edges, and Flat Patch Sampling to generate feasible navigation targets; code released. — [arXiv 2601.07718](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.07718)
- Other 2025 to 2026 humanoid work shows the same two options: CReF (March 2026) maps raw depth and proprioception end to end and reports more than 20 consecutive stair traversals, a 40 cm platform and an 80 cm gap — [arXiv 2603.29452](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2603.29452); the gait-adaptive framework (December 2025) reconstructs a dense egocentric height map from a downward depth camera with a U-Net and feeds that to the policy, crossing a 46 cm gap and doing stairs forwards and backwards — [arXiv 2512.07464](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.07464v1).
- Explicit semantic structure does exist at the map level: Spot's GraphNav edges carry stairs-mode and speed annotations — [Spot SDK](https://dev.bostondynamics.com/docs/concepts/autonomy/components_of_autonomous_navigation); a SubT planner tags OctoMap voxels "stair" for legged and "rough" for wheeled robots — [arXiv 2301.00771](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2301.00771); stair-specific perception outputs polygonal step planes for footstep planning — [arXiv 2510.12346](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.12346).

### Inferences
- The most transferable finding of this whole note: parkour policies are tiny. A 3-layer CNN, a 256-unit GRU and a [512, 256, 128] MLP at 50 Hz on a Jetson NX (Robot Parkour Learning) is on the order of a million parameters or fewer and would cost well under a millisecond on a desktop CPU in ONNX Runtime (my estimate; parameter counts are not published). If the bot ever needs a learned movement/affordance model, this is the size class, and it fits beside the 40 ms classifier.
- Because the engine supplies exact geometry, the bot can use the teacher representation directly and skip distillation: (a) scandots / height scans: a fixed grid or ring pattern of downward traces around and ahead of the agent; (b) Robot Parkour Learning's privileged tuple: distance to the next obstacle, its height and width, and a one-hot category (climb / leap / crawl / squeeze), which is effectively an affordance descriptor the engine can compute or designers can tag; (c) ANYmal Parkour's navigation output: skill index + local target + heading, which is a ready template for a "which traversal move, aimed where" head.
- The "semantic terrain" labels the question asks about (stairs, doors, ledges, gaps) are, in robotics, mostly derived or annotated rather than perceived by a dedicated public model: derived geometrically (plane segmentation for stairs, edge detection for ledges, missing ground for gaps) or attached to map edges. That matches game practice (nav links / smart objects) and suggests labelling structures at build time from geometry rather than running a perception network at runtime.
- Edge-awareness tricks carry over as scoring terms even without learning: penalize foot or target placements near detected terrain edges (2603.07928, Hiking in the Wild) and sample movement targets only on flat patches (Flat Patch Sampling).
- Not transferable: depth-image students, belief encoders, noise curricula and sim-to-real latency handling exist only to survive imperfect sensors.

### Gaps
- I found no public pretrained model whose output is a semantic terrain or affordance label map ("stairs / door / ledge / gap") intended for locomotion, with a license and a size that could be reported; the parkour repositories ship robot-specific RL policies. The licenses of the Extreme Parkour, Robot Parkour Learning and Hiking in the Wild code were not checked.
- The exact scandot grid of Extreme Parkour (number of points, spacing) and its network sizes are not given in the text read.
- Door affordances (opening, passing through) were not researched; the parkour literature does not cover them.
- No parameter counts are published for the parkour policies; the sizes above are inferred from the stated layer widths.

## 5. What is the typical division of labour between geometric perception, semantic perception and language in a modern robot stack?

### Takeaway
Modern stacks are layered by rate: language and semantics are slow (about 1 to 10 Hz, billions of parameters, GPU or cloud), geometric perception and local planning are mid-rate (5 to 30 Hz), and motor control is fast and tiny (50 Hz to 1 kHz, tens of millions of parameters or fewer). Language never touches geometry directly: it selects goals, sub-tasks or skills, and a geometry-aware layer makes them safe and executable.

### Cited Findings

- Figure Helix 02: three tiers. S2 (VLM; 7B at 7 to 9 Hz in the original Helix) does scene understanding and language and emits latent goals; S1 (80M in the original Helix) turns perception into full-body joint targets at 200 Hz; S0 (10M) outputs actuator commands at 1 kHz. — [Figure Helix](https://www.figure.ai/news/helix); [Figure Helix 02](https://www.figure.ai/news/helix-02)
- GR00T N1: System 2 is a 1.34B VLM at 10 Hz, System 1 a diffusion transformer generating actions at 120 Hz, 2.2B in total. — [arXiv 2503.14734](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.14734)
- Gemini Robotics: the embodied-reasoning model is a high-level planner that does not control actuators; it gives natural-language instructions per step and calls tools such as a VLA, Google Search or user-defined functions. — [Google DeepMind blog](https://deepmind.google/blog/gemini-robotics-15-brings-ai-agents-into-the-physical-world/); [Gemini API robotics docs](https://ai.google.dev/gemini-api/docs/robotics-overview)
- π0.7: a learned high-level policy (or a human) supplies subtask language and a world model supplies subgoal images; the VLA outputs 50-step action chunks; a PD controller with numerical inverse kinematics executes them. — [arXiv 2604.15483](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.15483)
- NaVILA: the VLA (8B, about 1 FPS) emits mid-level language actions; a separate RL locomotion policy with a LiDAR height map executes them and "ensures obstacle avoidance". — [arXiv 2412.04453](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.04453); [NaVILA GitHub](https://github.com/AnjieCheng/NaVILA)
- NavFoM: the 7B model on a remote RTX 4090 produces 8 waypoints in up to 0.5 s; the robot uploads observations asynchronously and local planners steer along the trajectory. — [arXiv 2509.12129](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.12129v2)
- ANYmal Parkour: asynchronous perception (voxel reconstruction), navigation policy at 5 Hz choosing skill and local target, locomotion skills at 50 Hz. — [arXiv 2306.14874](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2306.14874)
- ANYmal perceptive locomotion: elevation map at 20 Hz, policy at 50 Hz, with learned gating between proprioception and the map. — [arXiv 2201.08117](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2201.08117)
- Wild Visual Navigation: semantic visual features (DINO) feed a tiny learned traversability head, the result is projected onto the geometric elevation map, a reactive local planner outputs velocity commands, and a locomotion controller executes them. — [arXiv 2404.07110](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.07110)
- Spot: stairs handling and hazard avoidance are in the locomotion/local-grid layer; the mission layer (GraphNav) only annotates edges with stairs mode or speed limits. — [Spot SDK: robot services](https://dev.bostondynamics.com/docs/concepts/robot_services); [Spot SDK: components of autonomous navigation](https://dev.bostondynamics.com/docs/concepts/autonomy/components_of_autonomous_navigation)
- Survey framing (2025): dual-system VLAs "adopt two distinct systems: a larger and slower System 2 for high-level reasoning and planning, and a smaller and faster System 1 for low-level action generation", in cascade or parallel designs. — [arXiv 2508.13073](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2508.13073). The two systems usually exchange latent tokens or embeddings. — [arXiv 2510.17111](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.17111v1). See also the OpenHelix dual-system survey. — [arXiv 2505.03912](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.03912v1)

### Inferences
- A consolidated picture of the stack, with the game-bot equivalent:
  1. Language / task reasoning (VLM or ER model, 1 to 10 Hz or slower, GPU or cloud): picks the goal, sub-task or skill. Game equivalent: the existing intent classifier (40 ms, CPU). This is the only tier where a learned language model is needed, and it already exists.
  2. Semantic perception (what is this surface or object): on robots a foundation vision model plus a small head (WVN) or annotations on the map. Game equivalent: engine tags, physical materials, nav links, build-time labelling; no runtime network needed.
  3. Geometric perception (elevation / ESDF / no-step grids at 5 to 30 Hz, GPU on robots): Game equivalent: navmesh plus line traces and sweeps; free and exact.
  4. Local planning / skill selection (5 to 10 Hz): waypoints or a skill index plus local target. Game equivalent: path following plus a traversal-move selector; this is where a small learned model (parkour-policy size) or a hand-written scorer over local-grid features could add value.
  5. Motor control (50 Hz to 1 kHz, tiny networks): Game equivalent: the character movement component and animation; nothing to learn.
- The interface between tiers 1 and 4 is always narrow: a latent vector (Helix, GR00T), a short text command (NaVILA, Gemini ER to VLA), a handful of waypoints (NavFoM, ViNT), or a skill index plus target (ANYmal Parkour). For a CPU-only bot the discrete forms (command vocabulary, skill index + target point) are the practical ones.
- The robotics trend toward end-to-end VLAs is driven by the difficulty of perception and contact-rich control in the real world. With ground-truth state, the classical modular split is cheaper and more controllable, and the sources give no reason to expect an end-to-end model to beat it for a game bot.

### Gaps
- No single 2026 survey was found that lays out this layered architecture across navigation, locomotion and manipulation; the summary is assembled from individual system papers and vendor posts, and the rates quoted for Figure's S2/S1 come from the original Helix post, not from Helix 02.
- Vendor posts (Figure, Google DeepMind) are self-reported and not peer-reviewed; their numbers could not be independently verified.
- Whether the NVIDIA Isaac stack (Perceptor + GR00T) is deployed as one integrated layered system on any product was not researched.
